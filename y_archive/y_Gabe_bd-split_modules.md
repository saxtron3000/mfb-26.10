## **What Is Money?**

*"Money is a guarantee that we may have what we want in the future. Though we need nothing at the moment, it ensures the possibility of satisfying a new desire when it arises."* — Aristotle

Money is something most of us use every day without ever stopping to ask what it actually is.

At its most basic, money is a tool that makes trade easier. Without it, if you wanted something someone else had, you'd have to offer them something they wanted in return — at the same moment, in the right quantities. Money removes that constraint. It acts as a middle step that anyone can accept, hold, and pass along.

Think of money like a universal language. You can travel to a country where you don't speak a word of the local language, walk into a shop, place an item on the counter, and hand over the local currency. No words needed. Money communicates value across cultures, borders, and time.

It's worth being precise about what money is and isn't. Money doesn't have value in itself — a $20 bill is just paper. What gives it value is the shared agreement that it *represents* value and can be exchanged for things of real value: food, shelter, time, skills.

Money represents the value *by which* goods are exchanged. It is not the value *for which* goods are exchanged.

### **The Three Functions of Money**

For something to work as money, it needs to perform three jobs:

**Store of Value** — Money must hold its value over time. If you earn it today, you should be able to spend it next year and still get roughly the same amount in return. This is what makes saving possible — you're storing the value of your labor now to use it later.

**Medium of Exchange** — Money must be widely accepted so you can use it to buy anything from anyone. Without this, every transaction would require finding someone who has exactly what you need and wants exactly what you have — at the same time.

**Unit of Account** — Money gives us a common language for measuring value. Because everything is priced in the same unit, you can compare a concert ticket to a ski pass, a month's rent in one city to another, or your hourly wage to the cost of groceries. Without a shared unit of account, comparison becomes nearly impossible.

These three functions work together. When money fails at any one of them, the whole system starts to break down.

### **The Six Properties of Sound Money**

Not everything can function as money. Over thousands of years, humanity discovered that good money needs six specific properties:

**Durability** — Money must survive physical wear and tear. If it rots, melts, or falls apart, it becomes useless. A gold coin buried for 2,000 years comes out looking nearly the same as when it went in.

**Divisibility** — Money must be breakable into smaller units for transactions of any size. You can't hand over half a cow to pay for a loaf of bread.

**Portability** — Money must be easy to carry and transfer. A form of money that weighs 50 kilograms or can't cross a border isn't practical for everyday trade.

**Acceptability** — Money must be widely accepted. The more people and places that take it, the more useful it becomes. A currency only a handful of people recognize has limited power.

**Scarcity** — Money must be difficult to produce in large quantities. If anyone could easily make more, new supply would pour in and make every existing unit worth less. Scarcity protects value.

**Fungibility** — Every unit must be interchangeable with every other unit of the same denomination. One $10 bill is the same as any other $10 bill. One gram of gold equals any other gram of gold. If some units are seen as "better" than others, trust in the money collapses.

## **Money Has No Intrinsic Value**

One of the most important things to understand about money is that it has no value in itself.

A gold coin isn't valuable because gold is magical. It's valuable because people agree it is — because it's scarce, durable, and universally recognized. If society stopped agreeing, gold would just be a shiny metal.

Paper money is even clearer. A $100 bill costs a few cents to print. Its value comes entirely from trust: trust that other people will accept it, that the government backing it is stable, and that it won't lose its worth overnight.

This is why the *properties* of money matter so much. When something loses one of those properties — when it becomes too easy to produce, or people stop accepting it, or it can't hold value over time — it stops functioning as money, regardless of what a government declares.

History is full of examples. The Roman Empire debased its coins by mixing in cheaper metals until the currency collapsed. The Weimar Republic printed so much paper money after World War I that people needed wheelbarrows of cash to buy bread. Zimbabwe's dollar became worthless in the 2000s after the government printed money without limit.

The lesson isn't that money is fragile. It's that *bad money* is fragile. The properties of sound money exist for a reason.

## **The History of Money**

*"Money was not evolved by design but arose out of the market process. It was not created by governments. It emerged over time as a spontaneous order."* — Murray Rothbard

Money didn't begin as government-issued paper. It evolved, slowly and naturally, as human societies grew more complex and trade became more essential.

### **Phase 1: Barter — Why It Broke Down**

The earliest economies ran on direct exchange. You have fish, I have grain — we trade. No money required.

The problem is obvious once you think about it. For a trade to happen, both parties need to want exactly what the other has, in the right quantities, at the same moment. This is called the **double coincidence of wants**, and it made trade slow, frustrating, and increasingly impractical as societies grew.

As communities grew larger and more specialized, barter became a serious obstacle. You needed a better solution.

### **Phase 2: Commodity Money — Something Everyone Wants**

To solve the barter problem, societies began using intermediary goods — things that almost everyone wanted and would accept in trade, even if they didn't need them personally. Cattle, salt, shells, grain, and eventually precious metals like gold and silver emerged as natural forms of money.

These worked because they had real use value on their own, and because they were scarce — it took real effort to produce more of them. Gold was particularly well-suited: it doesn't corrode, it's easy to recognize, it's divisible into smaller pieces, and it's difficult to counterfeit. Most advanced civilizations eventually converged on gold and silver as their preferred money, not because a government told them to, but because it simply worked best.

### **Phase 3: Representative Money — Paper Backed by Gold**

Carrying gold and silver around was inconvenient, especially in large quantities. Banks emerged as a solution: they would store your gold and give you a paper receipt — a certificate you could exchange for a fixed amount of metal on demand.

This paper money was more portable and easier to divide. It represented real gold sitting in a vault. For a long time, it worked exactly as promised. You could hand your certificate to a bank and receive gold in return.

### **Phase 4: The Break from Gold — Why It Failed**

Here is where the history gets darker.

Banks discovered they could issue more certificates than they had gold to back them. Most people weren't redeeming their paper for gold at any given moment, so the gap was rarely noticed — until it was. This practice, lending more than you hold, would later become the foundation of the modern banking system.

Governments found the same trick useful. During wars and economic crises, they printed more money than their gold reserves could support. The gold standard became increasingly strained.

The final break came on August 15, 1971, when US President Nixon announced that the dollar would no longer be convertible to gold — ending the last link between money and a physical commodity. This is known as the **Nixon Shock**. Since that day, no major currency in the world has been backed by anything except trust in the government that issues it.

Each phase in this history failed or gave way for the same underlying reason: whoever controlled the money eventually found a way to create more of it for their own benefit, at the expense of everyone else who held it.

## **Today: The Fiat System**

*"The root problem with conventional currency is all the trust that's required to make it work. The central bank must be trusted not to debase the currency, but the history of fiat currencies is full of breaches of that trust."* — Satoshi Nakamoto

The money we use today is called **fiat money** — from the Latin word meaning "by decree." It has no intrinsic value and is not backed by any physical commodity. Its value comes entirely from government mandate and the public's willingness to accept it.

Every major currency in use today — dollars, euros, pesos, pounds, yen, naira — is fiat money.

### **Who Controls the Fiat System?**

**Central Banks** — Institutions like the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, and the Bank of Japan control the money supply. They set interest rates, regulate commercial banks, and act as a lender of last resort when the financial system is in trouble. They are not elected. They operate with significant independence from the public they affect.

**Commercial Banks** — Ordinary banks take deposits from customers and lend that money out to borrowers. They charge interest on loans, pay interest on deposits, and keep the difference as profit.

**Governments** — Governments fund their spending through taxes and by issuing bonds — essentially, IOUs to whoever buys them. When demand for those bonds is low, central banks step in to purchase them, effectively financing government spending by creating new money.

**Citizens** — Everyone who earns wages, pays taxes, and holds savings in the national currency. They are the last to benefit from new money creation and the first to feel its consequences.

### **Fractional Reserve Banking**

One of the most important — and least understood — features of the modern financial system is how banks create money.

When you deposit money in a bank, the bank doesn't keep it sitting in a vault. It lends most of it out. Banks are legally required to keep only a small *fraction* of deposits on hand as reserves — hence the term **fractional reserve banking**.

Here's the key: when a bank makes a loan, that loan becomes a new deposit somewhere else in the system. That deposit gets lent out again. And again. The same original deposit multiplies through the system, creating far more money than was originally deposited.

This means banks don't just store money — they *create* it. Every loan is new money added to the economy. And every loan repaid or defaulted on is money that disappears from the economy.

**The risk:** If too many depositors try to withdraw their money at the same time — a bank run — the bank cannot pay them all back. The money simply isn't there.

### **Inflation: The Devaluation of Money**

When the money supply grows faster than the amount of goods and services in the economy, prices rise. Each unit of money buys less than it did before. This is **inflation**, and it is a direct consequence of how the fiat system works.

Inflation isn't just rising prices. It's a transfer of wealth — invisible, automatic, and ongoing. Savers are punished. People who hold assets are protected. And those closest to the creation of new money — governments, banks, and large financial institutions — benefit before prices have had time to rise for everyone else.

The numbers tell the story. A dollar in 1913, when the US Federal Reserve was created, had roughly the same purchasing power as $30 today. What cost $1 then costs $30 now. The dollar didn't disappear — it just quietly lost 97% of its value over a century.

For ordinary people, this shows up as a treadmill effect. Wages rise, but so does everything else — often faster. You work harder and earn more in nominal terms, but your real purchasing power stagnates or falls. People respond rationally: spend now before money loses more value, take on debt to buy assets, save less.

The fiat system doesn't encourage long-term thinking. It punishes it.

## **Looking Ahead: CBDCs**

Central Bank Digital Currencies — CBDCs — are what many governments are building next.

A CBDC is a fully digital version of a national currency, issued directly by a central bank. Unlike the digital money in your bank account today, which is a claim on a commercial bank, a CBDC would be a direct claim on the government itself.

What makes CBDCs significant isn't the technology. It's the control they enable:

* Every transaction can be monitored in real time  
* Governments can program money to expire, restrict what it can buy, or block it from being spent in certain places  
* Accounts can be frozen or funds confiscated without involving a commercial bank at all

As of 2024, 134 countries representing 98% of global GDP are exploring CBDCs. Every G20 nation is in some stage of development or pilot.

CBDCs would take the existing problems of fiat money — unlimited supply, centralized control, surveillance of transactions — and intensify them. They are the logical endpoint of a system in which governments control money completely.

---

## **The Search for a Solution**

By the late 20th century, a group of computer scientists, mathematicians, and civil liberties advocates had been thinking carefully about these problems. They called themselves the **Cypherpunks**.

The Cypherpunks believed that cryptography — the mathematics of secure communication — could be a tool for individual freedom. They weren't interested in reforming the existing financial system. They wanted to build an alternative outside of it.

Their key insight: the problems with money were structural, not personal. As long as a small group of people controlled the money supply, they would eventually abuse that control. The solution wasn't better regulation or more trustworthy leaders. The solution was to remove the need for trust altogether.

### **Centralized vs. Decentralized**

To understand what the Cypherpunks were trying to build, it helps to understand the difference between centralized and decentralized systems.

**Centralized systems** have a single point of control. Traditional banks, payment networks, and fiat currencies are all centralized. When the central authority makes a decision — raises rates, freezes accounts, prints money, blocks a transaction — everyone in the system is affected and no one can opt out.

The strengths of centralized systems are efficiency and simplicity. The weaknesses are single points of failure and the inevitable concentration of power. Those at the center benefit most. Those at the edges have no recourse.

**Decentralized systems** distribute control across many participants. No single entity can override the rules. The system runs according to its code, not the decisions of any individual or institution.

Decentralized systems are harder to build and coordinate. But they are far more resistant to manipulation, censorship, and abuse of power.

### **Attempts at a Solution**

The Cypherpunks made several serious attempts to create digital money that didn't rely on banks or governments.

David Chaum created DigiCash in the 1980s — a system for private digital payments using cryptography. It was technically impressive but failed because it still required a central company to operate. When that company went bankrupt in 1998, DigiCash disappeared with it.

Adam Back created Hashcash in 1997 — a system that used computational work to prove that effort had been expended, a concept that would later become foundational to Bitcoin. But Hashcash was designed to fight email spam, not to create money.

Nick Szabo proposed Bit Gold in 1998 — arguably the closest precursor to Bitcoin. Bit Gold was designed to be scarce, decentralized, and impossible for any authority to inflate. It was never implemented, but many of its ideas survived.

Each attempt revealed something important about what was missing. A truly decentralized digital currency needed to solve a fundamental problem that had never been cracked: how do you prevent someone from spending the same digital money twice, without a central authority keeping track?

In 2008, someone solved it.

---

## **Key Takeaways**

* Money is a social technology — a tool that makes trade, saving, and planning possible at scale.  
* Good money performs three functions: store of value, medium of exchange, and unit of account.  
* Sound money requires six properties: durability, divisibility, portability, acceptability, scarcity, and fungibility.  
* Money has no intrinsic value. Its value comes from collective agreement and the properties that sustain it.  
* Money evolved from barter to commodity money to representative money to fiat — each transition driven by convenience, and each creating new vulnerabilities to manipulation.  
* Fiat money is not backed by anything physical. Its value rests on trust in governments that have repeatedly abused it.  
* Fractional reserve banking allows banks to create money through lending, expanding the money supply far beyond what was originally deposited.  
* Inflation is a transfer of wealth from savers to asset holders — invisible, automatic, and built into the fiat system.  
* CBDCs would extend the control of fiat money into programmable, fully monitored digital currency.  
* The Cypherpunks identified these problems and spent decades searching for a solution. Several came close. In 2008, one succeeded.

# Module 2: The Solution — How Bitcoin Works

## **Bitcoin Origins**

Module 1 ended in 2008, on the eve of a financial crisis that would expose every weakness of the fiat system in one moment. Banks had taken on catastrophic risk, knowing they would be bailed out if they failed. Millions of ordinary people lost homes, jobs, and savings. The institutions responsible were rescued with public money while their executives collected bonuses.

It was in this context — and not by coincidence — that Bitcoin appeared.

### **The Cryptography Mailing List**

On October 31, 2008, a message appeared on a small email list used by cryptographers and computer scientists to discuss ideas in their field. The sender used the name Satoshi Nakamoto. The message was brief. It linked to a nine-page document titled *Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System* and described it in a single sentence: a system for electronic transactions without relying on trust.

The whitepaper solved a problem that had defeated every previous attempt at digital money: how do you prevent someone from spending the same digital coin twice, without a central authority keeping track? Every prior system had answered that question by appointing a trusted third party — a company, a bank, a server — to maintain the ledger. Nakamoto's answer was different. No trusted third party. The ledger would be maintained by everyone, simultaneously, according to rules enforced by mathematics.

Most of the cryptographers on the list were skeptical. A few were intrigued.

### **The Timeline**

| Date | Event |
| ----- | ----- |
| October 31, 2008 | Satoshi Nakamoto publishes the Bitcoin whitepaper on the cryptography mailing list |
| January 3, 2009 | The Genesis Block is mined — the first block in the Bitcoin blockchain, containing a message referencing a newspaper headline about bank bailouts |
| January 12, 2009 | The first Bitcoin transaction: Nakamoto sends 10 bitcoin to cryptographer Hal Finney |
| May 22, 2010 | The first real-world purchase: 10,000 bitcoin exchanged for two pizzas in Jacksonville, Florida — now celebrated annually as Bitcoin Pizza Day |
| 2011 | Nakamoto steps away from the project, leaving it in the hands of the open-source community |

The message embedded in the Genesis Block read: *"The Times 03/Jan/2009 Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks."* It wasn't accidental. Bitcoin was a direct response to a system that had just proven it could not be trusted.

Nakamoto's identity has never been confirmed. Whether Satoshi is a person or a group, they chose not to claim credit, not to hold a privileged position in the network, and not to profit from the invention in any traceable way. In doing so, they gave Bitcoin something no prior digital currency had: a genuinely leaderless foundation.

---

## **What Is Bitcoin?**

Bitcoin is a lot of things, and people describe it differently depending on what they find most important about it. But at its core, Bitcoin is **a new form of money** — one designed to address every structural flaw of the fiat system we covered in Module 1\.

Four properties define what makes Bitcoin different from anything that came before it:

**Decentralized** — No company, government, bank, or individual controls Bitcoin. It runs on a global network of computers, each independently enforcing the same rules. There is no headquarters, no CEO, no off switch.

**Digital** — Bitcoin exists only as information. It has no physical form. This makes it borderless by nature — it moves as easily between continents as it does between people in the same room.

**Peer-to-peer** — Transactions happen directly between sender and recipient, with no bank or payment processor in the middle. There is no intermediary to approve, delay, reverse, or block a transaction.

**Finite** — There will only ever be 21 million bitcoin. This limit is written into Bitcoin's code and enforced by every participant in the network. No government can inflate it. No central bank can print more. It is the first form of money in history with an absolutely fixed supply.

These aren't just technical features. They are direct answers to the problems in Module 1\. Decentralization addresses the abuse of centralized control. The fixed supply addresses inflation. Peer-to-peer removes the intermediaries who extract fees and can censor transactions. Digitality removes borders.

---

## **What Are Peers?**

Bitcoin is described as a peer-to-peer system. Understanding what that means is important.

A **peer** is simply an equal participant. In a peer-to-peer system, there is no central authority, no hierarchy, and no one whose permission you need. Every participant has the same standing in the network.

This is different from how most systems work. When you use a bank, you are not a peer — you are a customer. The bank holds your money, processes your transactions, and can refuse to do either. You operate within rules you didn't write and can't change.

In Bitcoin's peer-to-peer network, you hold your own money. You initiate your own transactions. The network validates them according to rules that apply equally to everyone, including the wealthiest institutions and the most powerful governments. No one gets special access or special treatment.

---

## **The Participants: Who Keeps Bitcoin Running?**

Bitcoin has no employees. No one is paid to maintain it in the traditional sense. Instead, it is sustained by a network of participants, each playing a distinct role, each with their own incentives to keep the system running honestly.

### **Users**

Users are anyone who holds or transacts in bitcoin. This includes individuals saving in bitcoin, merchants accepting it as payment, and anyone sending money across borders. Users interact with Bitcoin through wallets — software that manages their keys and allows them to send and receive.

Users don't need to understand how Bitcoin works at a technical level to use it, just as you don't need to understand TCP/IP to send an email. But understanding the basics of who else is in the network helps explain why it can be trusted.

### **Nodes**

A node is a computer running Bitcoin software that holds a complete copy of the Bitcoin ledger — every transaction ever made, all the way back to the Genesis Block in 2009\.

Nodes are the network's enforcement mechanism. Every time a new transaction is broadcast or a new block is proposed by a miner, nodes independently check it against the rules. If a transaction violates any rule — if someone tries to spend more bitcoin than they have, or create bitcoin out of thin air — nodes reject it. They don't ask anyone's permission. They simply apply the rules.

Anyone can run a node. You don't need authorization. The more nodes exist around the world, the harder it becomes for any individual or group to subvert the network, because they would need to overpower not one central server but thousands of independent computers simultaneously.

### **Miners**

Miners are the participants who add new transactions to the blockchain. They do this through a process called **Proof of Work** — using specialized computers to perform enormous numbers of calculations in competition with each other, racing to solve a mathematical puzzle. The first miner to solve it earns the right to add the next block of transactions to the blockchain, and receives newly created bitcoin as a reward.

Mining is what makes the blockchain secure. Altering a past transaction would require redoing all the computational work that went into every block since that transaction — an astronomical amount of energy and computing power. The cost of attacking Bitcoin makes honest participation far more profitable than cheating.

Miners are distributed around the world. No single miner or mining company controls the network.

### **The Symbiosis**

These three groups — users, nodes, and miners — form an interdependent system. Users create transactions. Miners collect and record them. Nodes verify everything. Each group needs the others for Bitcoin to function.

No single participant has enough power to override the others. Users can't spend money they don't have because nodes reject invalid transactions. Miners can't create extra bitcoin for themselves because nodes enforce the supply rules. No government or corporation can shut Bitcoin down because there is no central point to attack.

This is what decentralization actually means in practice: not just that control is distributed, but that the system's rules are enforced by the collective rather than by any authority.

---

## **How Does a Transaction Work?**

When you send bitcoin to someone, here is what actually happens:

1. **You create a transaction** — Your wallet software constructs a message that says, in effect: "The owner of this bitcoin authorizes transferring X amount to this address." You sign it with your private key — a cryptographic signature that proves ownership without revealing your key to anyone.  
2. **The transaction is broadcast** — Your wallet sends the transaction out to the network, where it is picked up by nodes and passed along to other nodes until it has spread across the entire network.  
3. **Nodes validate it** — Every node that receives the transaction checks it against Bitcoin's rules. Does the sender actually own the bitcoin they're trying to spend? Is the signature valid? If anything is wrong, the transaction is rejected and goes no further.  
4. **Miners include it in a block** — Valid transactions wait in a queue called the mempool until a miner selects them and includes them in a block. Transactions with higher fees are typically prioritized.  
5. **The block is added to the chain** — When a miner wins the Proof of Work competition, their block is broadcast to the network. Nodes verify the block is valid and add it to their copy of the blockchain. The transaction is now confirmed.  
6. **Ownership transfers** — The bitcoin is now associated with the recipient's address. Only someone with the corresponding private key can spend it next.

The entire process typically takes about ten minutes for the first confirmation. Each subsequent block added on top of it makes the transaction more permanent. After a few confirmations, reversing a transaction is computationally impossible for any realistic attacker.

## **How Do We Know There Will Only Ever Be 21 Million Bitcoin?**

This is one of the most important questions anyone new to Bitcoin asks. The answer lies in Bitcoin's code — and in the structure of its incentive system.

Bitcoin's rules specify exactly how much new bitcoin can be created and when. These rules are not controlled by any person or organization. They are enforced by every node in the network simultaneously. If someone tried to create a version of Bitcoin that produced more than 21 million coins, every honest node in the world would simply reject it as invalid.

### **The Block Reward**

When a miner successfully adds a block to the blockchain, they receive a reward in newly created bitcoin. This is called the **block reward**, and it is the only mechanism by which new bitcoin enters circulation. There is no other way to create bitcoin. No government can authorize more. No company can issue more. The code controls it.

### **The Halving**

Every 210,000 blocks — approximately every four years — the block reward is cut in half. This event is called **the Halving**.

| Year | Block Reward |
| ----- | ----- |
| 2009 | 50 BTC |
| 2012 | 25 BTC |
| 2016 | 12.5 BTC |
| 2020 | 6.25 BTC |
| 2024 | 3.125 BTC |
| 2028 | 1.5625 BTC |

Each halving reduces the rate at which new bitcoin enters circulation, making bitcoin increasingly scarce over time. The halvings continue until approximately the year 2140, at which point the last fraction of a bitcoin will have been mined and the total supply will reach — but never exceed — 21 million.

After that point, miners will earn only transaction fees, which are expected to sustain the network as Bitcoin's adoption grows.

The Halving is not a decision made by anyone. It is written into Bitcoin's code and will execute automatically, as it always has, regardless of who wants it to or doesn't.

### **The Supply Equation**

Bitcoin's total supply follows a mathematically precise schedule. The formula is a geometric series that converges on 21 million:

Total supply \= 50 \+ 25 \+ 12.5 \+ 6.25 \+ ... (continuing to halve indefinitely)

This series never quite reaches 21 million due to the mathematics of infinite halvings, but it approaches it asymptotically. In practice, Bitcoin's smallest unit — the satoshi — means the supply will effectively reach its maximum around 2140\.

As of 2025, approximately 19.8 million of the 21 million bitcoin have already been mined. Over 94% of all bitcoin that will ever exist already exists.

## **Am I Too Late? BTC \= Sats**

One of the most common reactions people have when they first learn about Bitcoin is: *I missed it. It's too expensive now.*

This reaction comes from thinking about bitcoin as something you have to buy a whole unit of. You don't.

Bitcoin is divisible into 100,000,000 units called **satoshis**, or **sats**. One bitcoin equals 100 million sats. You can buy, hold, and transact in any fraction of a bitcoin — down to a single satoshi if you choose.

| Unit | Amount |
| ----- | ----- |
| 1 bitcoin (BTC) | 100,000,000 satoshis |
| 0.01 BTC | 1,000,000 satoshis |
| 0.0001 BTC | 10,000 satoshis |
| 0.00000001 BTC | 1 satoshi |

Thinking in sats removes the psychological barrier of price. You don't need to own a whole bitcoin to participate in the network. You can start with whatever you have.

Whether you are "too late" is a different question — and one that depends on why you're interested in Bitcoin in the first place. The fixed supply means that as more people adopt Bitcoin, each unit represents a larger share of a finite resource. That dynamic doesn't have an expiration date.

## **Bitcoin as Sound Money**

In Module 1, you scored various forms of money against the six properties of sound money: durability, divisibility, portability, acceptability, scarcity, and fungibility. You were asked to leave Bitcoin blank.

Now fill it in.

**Durability** — Bitcoin is purely digital and immune to physical deterioration. It cannot rust, rot, burn, or wear out. As long as the network exists, every bitcoin that was ever created still exists exactly as it was.

**Divisibility** — Bitcoin can be divided into 100 million units. The US dollar divides to the cent — one hundredth of a dollar. Bitcoin divides to the satoshi — one hundred-millionth of a bitcoin. It is the most divisible monetary asset ever created.

**Portability** — In July 2025, over $1 billion worth of bitcoin was transferred in minutes for a fee of approximately $10. No other payment system in the world can move that amount of value that quickly, at that cost, without intermediaries. Bitcoin can be carried across any border in your head, as a memorized seed phrase.

**Acceptability** — Bitcoin is still in early adoption. It is not yet accepted everywhere. This is Bitcoin's weakest property today — and the one most likely to improve as adoption grows.

**Scarcity** — There will never be more than 21 million bitcoin. No authority can change this. Bitcoin is the first asset in human history with a supply that is both absolutely fixed and publicly verifiable by anyone.

**Fungibility** — Each unit of bitcoin is identical to every other unit and can be freely exchanged on a like-for-like basis.

## **Personal Responsibility**

Bitcoin gives you something the fiat system never did: full control of your own money. No bank can freeze your account. No government can inflate away your savings. No payment processor can block your transaction.

But this comes with a trade-off that matters: **there is no one to call if something goes wrong.**

In the fiat world, if you lose access to your bank account, you call customer support. If your card is stolen, the bank can reverse the charges. The system absorbs your mistakes.

Bitcoin has no customer support. If you lose your private key — the cryptographic credential that proves ownership of your bitcoin — you lose access to your bitcoin permanently. There is no recovery process, no appeal, no exception.

This isn't a flaw. It's a feature of a system that has no central authority to appeal to — which is precisely what makes it resistant to censorship and control. But it means that using Bitcoin responsibly requires understanding what you're holding and taking that responsibility seriously.

We'll cover the practical side of this — wallets, keys, and how to keep your bitcoin safe — in Module 3\.

## **Key Takeaways**

* Bitcoin was created by Satoshi Nakamoto in 2008, published as a whitepaper on a cryptography mailing list, and launched as a live network on January 3, 2009\.  
* Bitcoin is decentralized, digital, peer-to-peer, and finite — properties that directly address the failures of the fiat system.  
* The network is sustained by three groups of participants: users, nodes, and miners, each playing a distinct and interdependent role.  
* Transactions are cryptographically signed, broadcast to the network, validated by nodes, recorded by miners, and permanently added to the blockchain.  
* There will only ever be 21 million bitcoin. This limit is enforced by code, not by any authority, and cannot be changed without the consensus of the entire network.  
* The Halving cuts the block reward in half every four years, progressively reducing the rate of new supply until the last bitcoin is mined around 2140\.  
* Bitcoin is divisible into 100 million satoshis. You do not need to own a whole bitcoin to participate.  
* Bitcoin scores highly on every property of sound money — with acceptability being the one area still developing as adoption grows.  
* Full control of your money means full responsibility for it. There is no safety net, and no one to call.

## **How Accounts Work in Bitcoin**

In the traditional financial system, an account is something a bank creates for you. The bank holds your money, maintains a record of your balance, and grants you access to it. You don't hold the money — you hold a claim on the money, and the bank can restrict or revoke that claim.

Bitcoin works differently. There are no accounts in the traditional sense, and there is no institution that holds your bitcoin on your behalf — unless you choose one. Instead, ownership of bitcoin comes down to one thing: **control of keys**.

To understand how this works, you need to understand three concepts: addresses, keys, and seed phrases.

### **Addresses**

A Bitcoin address is like a mailbox. It's a string of letters and numbers — something like `bc1qxy2kgdygjrsqtzq2n0yrf249` — that represents a destination on the Bitcoin network. Anyone can see an address. Anyone can send bitcoin to it. But only the person who holds the corresponding private key can spend the bitcoin inside it.

You can generate as many addresses as you want. In fact, good practice is to use a fresh address for each incoming transaction, which improves privacy by making it harder to trace your activity on the public blockchain.

### **Keys**

Every Bitcoin address has two associated keys: a **public key** and a **private key**.

The **public key** is derived from the private key through a mathematical process that cannot be reversed. It is used to generate your address and can be shared freely with anyone who wants to send you bitcoin.

The **private key** is the master credential. It is a long random number — effectively a secret — that proves you own the bitcoin at a given address and authorizes you to spend it. When you send bitcoin, your wallet uses your private key to create a digital signature that the network can verify. Without the private key, the bitcoin at that address cannot be moved by anyone.

The relationship between these two keys is foundational to how Bitcoin works:

* Your public key is like your email address — you share it so people can reach you  
* Your private key is like your email password — you never share it with anyone

If someone obtains your private key, they have complete and irreversible access to the bitcoin associated with it. There is no way to freeze the account, reverse transactions, or recover funds. This is why protecting your private key is the most important thing you will learn in this module.

### **The Seed Phrase**

A private key is a large random number — not something a human can easily remember or write down reliably. To make backup and recovery practical, Bitcoin wallets use a **seed phrase** (also called a recovery phrase).

A seed phrase is a list of 12 or 24 ordinary words — chosen from a standardized list of 2,048 words — that encodes your private key in a human-readable form. For example:

*witch collapse practice feed shame open despair creek road again ice least*

This sequence of words is everything. It can be used to regenerate your private key and restore full access to your bitcoin on any compatible wallet, on any device, anywhere in the world. Whoever holds the seed phrase holds the bitcoin — full stop.

This means:

* If you lose your seed phrase and lose access to your device, your bitcoin is gone permanently  
* If someone else finds your seed phrase, they can take your bitcoin instantly and irrecoverably  
* No company, no wallet provider, and no government can help you recover it

The seed phrase must be written down on paper — not stored in a photo, a cloud document, a text message, or anywhere digital — and kept somewhere safe, private, and backed up. This is not optional. It is the single most important security practice in Bitcoin.

---

## **What Is a Bitcoin Wallet?**

Despite the name, a Bitcoin wallet does not actually store bitcoin. Bitcoin never leaves the blockchain. What a wallet stores is your **private key** — and what it does is give you an interface to interact with your bitcoin on the network.

A Bitcoin wallet does three things:

**Shows your balance** — The wallet reads the blockchain and displays how much bitcoin is associated with your addresses.

**Receives bitcoin** — The wallet generates addresses for you to share with senders. When bitcoin is sent to one of your addresses, your balance updates.

**Sends bitcoin** — The wallet uses your private key to sign transactions and broadcast them to the network, transferring ownership of your bitcoin to someone else's address.

The wallet is the interface. Your keys are what actually give you ownership. This distinction matters: you can move from one wallet application to another at any time, as long as you have your seed phrase. The bitcoin isn't in the app — it's on the blockchain, accessible to whoever holds the keys.

---

## **Custodial vs. Self-Custodial**

The most important decision you'll make in Bitcoin is whether to use a **custodial** or **self-custodial** wallet. This choice determines whether you actually own your bitcoin — or just have a claim on it.

### **Custodial Wallets**

A custodial wallet is one where a third party — typically an exchange or a financial company — holds your private keys on your behalf. You log in with a username and password, see a balance, and can initiate transactions. But the company controls the keys, which means the company controls the bitcoin.

This is how most people first encounter bitcoin. Buying on a major exchange like Coinbase or Binance gives you a custodial balance. It's convenient, familiar, and easy to recover if you forget your password.

But it comes with risks most people underestimate:

* The company can freeze your account at any time, for any reason  
* The company can be hacked, and your bitcoin can be stolen  
* The company can become insolvent and lose customer funds — this has happened repeatedly, most famously with FTX in 2022, where customers lost billions  
* Governments can compel the company to seize your funds or block withdrawals

The popular saying in Bitcoin is: **"Not your keys, not your coins."** If you don't control the private key, you don't truly own the bitcoin. You own an IOU from whoever does.

### **Self-Custodial Wallets**

A self-custodial wallet is one where you hold your own private keys. No company has access to your bitcoin. No one can freeze your account, seize your funds, or block your transactions. You are your own bank.

This comes with the full weight of responsibility that implies. If you lose your seed phrase, no one can help you recover your bitcoin. There is no customer support line. There is no appeal process. The tradeoff is real: more control, more responsibility.

For most people, the right approach is to start with a small amount in a self-custodial wallet and build familiarity gradually. The goal is eventually to hold your own keys for any meaningful amount of bitcoin.

### **Hot vs. Cold**

Within self-custody, there is a further distinction based on whether your wallet is connected to the internet.

**Hot wallets** are connected to the internet — typically mobile or desktop apps. They are convenient for everyday use and small amounts. Because they are online, they carry a higher risk of being compromised by malware or hackers.

**Cold wallets** (also called hardware wallets) store your private keys on a dedicated physical device that never connects to the internet. To sign a transaction, you connect the device briefly and confirm on its screen — your private key never touches an internet-connected computer. Cold wallets are significantly more secure for storing larger amounts.

The general principle: keep small amounts in a hot wallet for spending, and larger savings in cold storage. The same logic applies to cash — you carry a little in your pocket and keep the rest somewhere safer.

|  | Custodial | Self-Custodial (Hot) | Self-Custodial (Cold) |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Who holds the keys | Third party | You | You |
| Internet connected | Yes | Yes | No |
| Ease of use | High | Medium | Lower |
| Security | Low | Medium | High |
| Recovery if lost | Contact support | Seed phrase | Seed phrase |
| Risk of seizure | Yes | No | No |

## **Setting Up a Self-Custodial Wallet**

Setting up a self-custodial wallet for the first time follows the same sequence regardless of which app you use.

**Step 1 — Choose a wallet**

Look for a wallet that is open-source (the code is publicly auditable), has a strong reputation in the Bitcoin community, and is compatible with your device. Bitcoin.org/en/choose-your-wallet is a good starting point. For this module, a mobile wallet is sufficient.

**Step 2 — Install and create a new wallet**

Download the app from your device's official app store. Open it and select the option to create a new wallet. The app will generate a private key for you automatically — you don't need to do anything technical.

**Step 3 — Write down your seed phrase**

This is the most important step. The wallet will display your seed phrase — 12 or 24 words in a specific order. Write every word down by hand, in order, on paper. Do not screenshot it. Do not type it into any other app or document. Store this paper somewhere safe and private. Consider making a second copy stored in a different location.

The wallet will ask you to confirm your seed phrase by entering the words back in order. This is to make sure you wrote it down correctly.

**Step 4 — Set a PIN or passphrase**

Most wallets allow you to set a PIN or password for the app itself. This protects your wallet if someone picks up your phone, but it is not a substitute for securing your seed phrase. The PIN unlocks the app. The seed phrase unlocks the bitcoin.

**Step 5 — Your wallet is ready**

Once set up, your wallet will show a balance of zero and be ready to receive bitcoin.

## **Receiving Bitcoin**

To receive bitcoin, you share your address with whoever is sending to you.

Open your wallet and look for a **Receive** button. Your wallet will display your current receiving address, usually as both a string of text and a QR code. Either can be shared — the QR code is convenient when the sender is physically nearby and can scan it with their phone.

A few things worth knowing:

* Bitcoin addresses are case-sensitive. A single wrong character means the transaction goes to a different address — or no valid address at all  
* You can generate a fresh address for each transaction you receive. Your wallet manages all of these addresses and shows you a combined balance  
* Once bitcoin is sent to your address, it will appear in your wallet after the transaction receives one confirmation — typically within ten minutes

## **Recovering a Wallet**

At some point, you may need to restore your wallet — if you get a new phone, the app is deleted, or the device is lost. This is exactly what the seed phrase is for.

On any compatible Bitcoin wallet app, look for an option to **import** or **restore** an existing wallet rather than creating a new one. You will be prompted to enter your seed phrase words in order. Once confirmed, the wallet will scan the blockchain and restore your full balance and transaction history.

This is also how you can move from one wallet app to another — not by transferring the bitcoin, but by importing your seed phrase into the new app. The bitcoin stays on the blockchain. Only the interface changes.

**Test this before you need to.** After setting up your wallet, consider restoring it on a second device or app to confirm your seed phrase works correctly. Discovering a transcription error when it's urgent is far worse than discovering it deliberately.

## **Sending Bitcoin**

Sending bitcoin is straightforward in practice, but deserves care because **Bitcoin transactions are irreversible**. Once a transaction is broadcast to the network and confirmed, it cannot be undone by anyone — not by you, not by the recipient, and not by any authority.

To send bitcoin:

1. Open your wallet and tap **Send**  
2. Enter the recipient's address — either by typing it, pasting it, or scanning their QR code  
3. Enter the amount you want to send, in bitcoin or in your local currency equivalent  
4. Set a transaction fee — higher fees get confirmed faster; most wallets will suggest an appropriate fee automatically  
5. Review the details carefully: the address, the amount, and the fee  
6. Confirm and broadcast

Before confirming, double-check the recipient's address. The standard practice is to verify at least the first and last several characters. Many people copy-paste addresses, but malware exists that silently replaces copied addresses with an attacker's address. Verifying manually protects against this.

Once sent, the transaction enters the mempool and will typically be confirmed within ten minutes. You can track its status on any Bitcoin block explorer — a public tool that shows the current state of every transaction on the blockchain.

## **Don't Trust, Verify**

Bitcoin operates on a principle that is unusual in the financial world: you don't have to trust anyone's claims. You can verify everything yourself.

Every transaction ever made on Bitcoin is recorded publicly on the blockchain. Anyone can download a copy of it. Anyone can run a node and independently verify that every rule has been followed. You don't have to trust that there are only 21 million bitcoin — you can verify it. You don't have to trust that your transaction was confirmed — you can look it up.

This principle extends to how you should approach everything in the Bitcoin space. The ecosystem attracts scammers, promoters of dubious projects, and people who will make extraordinary claims about returns and opportunities. The antidote is the same in every case: don't trust, verify.

Specifically:

* **Verify addresses before every transaction** — never send to an address you haven't confirmed is correct  
* **Be skeptical of anyone promising guaranteed returns** — Bitcoin doesn't work that way, and no one can guarantee it  
* **Prefer open-source wallets** — if the code is public, the community can audit it for security flaws; closed-source wallets ask you to simply trust the developer  
* **Verify your seed phrase works** before you store significant amounts  
* **Be suspicious of urgency** — scams often create artificial time pressure to prevent you from thinking carefully

Bitcoin gives you full control of your money. Protecting that control requires the same critical thinking that Bitcoin itself is built on.

## **Bitcoin for Daily Use**

The previous three modules built the foundation. You understand why the fiat system has structural problems, what Bitcoin is and how it works, and how to hold your own keys. This module is about using Bitcoin in the real world — for everyday payments, for buying and selling, and for participating in a growing global economy that runs on sound money.

The challenge is that Bitcoin, as powerful as it is, has a limitation when it comes to everyday transactions. Understanding that limitation — and how it has been solved — is where this module begins.

---

## **The Scaling Problem**

Bitcoin's base layer processes transactions slowly and deliberately. Each block takes roughly ten minutes to confirm, and the network handles approximately seven transactions per second globally. This is not a bug. It is a deliberate tradeoff in favor of security and decentralization — every transaction is verified by thousands of independent nodes around the world, and that process takes time.

For storing value or sending large amounts, this is perfectly acceptable. Waiting ten minutes to confirm a significant transfer is not a problem. But for buying a coffee, paying for a meal, or splitting a bill with a friend, it doesn't work. Nobody wants to wait ten minutes at a register, and the transaction fees that fluctuate with network demand make small payments impractical when fees are high.

This is the **scaling problem**: Bitcoin's base layer, optimized for security and finality, is not suited for the speed and volume that everyday commerce requires.

The solution isn't to change Bitcoin's base layer — doing so would compromise the security and decentralization that make it valuable. Instead, the solution is to build on top of it.

---

## **Lightning as a Solution**

The **Lightning Network** is a second layer built on top of Bitcoin. It enables fast, cheap, near-instant payments without sacrificing Bitcoin's underlying security.

The core idea: instead of recording every transaction on the blockchain, two parties open a private payment channel between them and conduct as many transactions as they want off-chain. Only two transactions ever touch the blockchain — one to open the channel and one to close it — regardless of how many payments happen in between. The final balance is what settles on-chain.

Think of it like running a tab at a local café. You don't pay separately for each coffee during the week. You settle the full tab at the end. In between, every transaction between you and the café happens instantly and without fees, because you've already established mutual trust through the initial deposit.

The Lightning Network scales this concept across thousands of interconnected channels. You don't need a direct channel with everyone you want to pay. Payments route automatically through the network — from your channel to an intermediate node to the recipient — arriving in seconds for a fraction of a cent in fees.

|  | Bitcoin On-Chain | Lightning Network |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Speed | \~10 minutes | Seconds |
| Fees | Variable, can be high | Fractions of a cent |
| Security | Maximum | High |
| Best for | Large transfers, savings | Everyday payments |
| Transactions per second | \~7 | Millions (theoretical) |

For comparison, Visa processes roughly 65,000 transactions per second. Bitcoin's base layer handles 7\. The Lightning Network is theoretically capable of millions — making it competitive with any payment network in the world, while remaining rooted in a sound money system.

---

## **Using a Custodial Lightning Wallet**

For everyday spending, a custodial Lightning wallet is often the right starting point. The tradeoffs of custodial wallets discussed in Module 3 apply here too — but for small amounts used in daily transactions, convenience reasonably outweighs the custody risk.

The setup is simpler than a self-custodial on-chain wallet. Most custodial Lightning wallets require only:

1. Download the app  
2. Create an account (email or username, no seed phrase required — the provider holds the keys)  
3. Add some sats by receiving from another wallet or buying directly in-app where available

Once set up, sending and receiving on Lightning works through **invoices** and **Lightning addresses**.

**To receive:** Your wallet generates an invoice — a payment request that encodes the amount and destination. Share it as a QR code or a text string. The sender scans or pastes it and the payment arrives in seconds.

**To send:** Scan or paste the recipient's invoice, confirm the amount, and send. The payment routes through the network and arrives near-instantly. No address to mistype, no ten-minute wait.

Lightning addresses are an increasingly common alternative — they look like email addresses (e.g. `yourname@wallet.provider`) and work as permanent, reusable payment destinations without generating a new invoice each time.

## **Buying and Selling Bitcoin**

Getting bitcoin into your wallet is the practical prerequisite for everything in this module. There are several ways to do it, each with different tradeoffs around convenience, privacy, and cost.

### **KYC vs. No KYC**

The most important distinction when buying bitcoin is whether the purchase requires **KYC** — Know Your Customer — identity verification.

KYC is a regulatory requirement imposed on financial institutions that obligates them to collect and verify the identity of their customers. In practice, this means providing your name, address, date of birth, government ID, and often a selfie before you can buy bitcoin through that platform.

**KYC bitcoin** is bitcoin purchased through a platform that has collected your identity. The platform knows how much you bought, when, and at what price. This information may be shared with tax authorities, law enforcement, or third parties depending on the jurisdiction and the platform's policies. If the platform is hacked, your personal data is at risk. If the platform is compelled to restrict withdrawals or freeze accounts, your ability to access your bitcoin depends on their cooperation.

**No-KYC bitcoin** is bitcoin acquired without providing identity information — through peer-to-peer trades, certain Bitcoin ATMs, mining, or accepting it as payment for goods or services. It preserves financial privacy. There is no central record linking your identity to your holdings.

Neither option is inherently right for everyone. KYC platforms are more convenient and often offer better prices and liquidity. No-KYC methods take more effort and may involve a premium. The choice depends on your priorities and your local regulatory environment.

A practical consideration: in many countries, bitcoin purchases are taxable events and must be reported. The method you use to acquire bitcoin doesn't change your legal obligations. Understanding your local tax laws matters regardless of whether you use KYC or non-KYC methods.

### **Exchanges vs. Peer-to-Peer**

**Centralized exchanges** are companies — Coinbase, Kraken, Binance, and others — that act as intermediaries between buyers and sellers. They offer convenience, high liquidity, and familiar interfaces. They almost universally require KYC.

The risks are the same as any custodial relationship: the exchange holds your bitcoin until you withdraw it. Several major exchanges have failed, been hacked, or frozen customer withdrawals over the years. The rule from Module 3 applies: as soon as you buy bitcoin on an exchange, withdraw it to your own wallet.

**Peer-to-peer (P2P)** platforms connect individual buyers and sellers directly without a company in the middle. Platforms like Bisq, Peach, and HodlHodl facilitate trades between people, often with escrow mechanisms to reduce the risk of fraud. Many P2P platforms offer no-KYC options or have lower identity requirements than centralized exchanges.

P2P trades can also happen entirely in person — you hand cash to someone, they send bitcoin to your wallet. This is the most privacy-preserving method and requires no platform at all, though it carries the usual risks of any cash transaction with a stranger.

|  | Centralized Exchange | P2P Platform | In-Person P2P |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| KYC required | Usually yes | Often optional | No |
| Convenience | High | Medium | Low |
| Privacy | Low | Medium | High |
| Custody risk | High (until withdrawal) | Low | None |
| Liquidity | High | Medium | Varies |

### **Other Ways to Acquire Bitcoin**

Buying isn't the only way to get bitcoin:

**Earn it** — Accept bitcoin as payment for work, goods, or services. This is the most direct path and avoids any exchange entirely. Some employers pay salaries fully or partially in bitcoin.

**Bitcoin ATMs** — Physical machines that accept cash and send bitcoin to your wallet. They are widely available in many cities, typically require minimal or no KYC for small amounts, but charge higher fees than exchanges — often 5–10%.

**Gift card services** — Services like Bitrefill allow you to purchase gift cards for major retailers using bitcoin. The reverse also applies: some platforms allow you to trade gift cards for bitcoin.

## **Best Practices and Things to Watch Out For**

Bitcoin gives you full control of your money. That also means full exposure to your own mistakes — and to the mistakes of others who want to take your bitcoin. The following principles have protected countless people and cost others dearly when ignored.

### **Never Share Your Seed Phrase**

Your seed phrase is your bitcoin. Anyone who has it can take everything. No legitimate wallet, exchange, support team, or developer will ever ask for it. If anyone asks for your seed phrase for any reason, it is a scam. Full stop.

### **Withdraw From Exchanges Promptly**

Exchanges are not banks and are not insured the way banks are in most countries. Once you have bought bitcoin on an exchange, withdraw it to a wallet where you hold your own keys. Leaving bitcoin on an exchange is accepting the custody risk of that company indefinitely.

### **Verify Addresses Before Every Transaction**

Bitcoin transactions are irreversible. If you send to the wrong address, the bitcoin is gone. Always double-check the first and last several characters of any address before confirming a send. Be aware that malware exists specifically to replace copied addresses with an attacker's address — verify manually, especially for significant amounts.

### **Be Skeptical of Too-Good-To-Be-True Offers**

Scams in the Bitcoin space follow predictable patterns:

* **Giveaway scams**: "Send 0.1 BTC and receive 0.2 BTC back." No legitimate giveaway works this way.  
* **Investment schemes**: Promises of guaranteed returns or "managed" bitcoin accounts. Bitcoin's price is volatile and no one can guarantee returns.  
* **Impersonation**: Fake customer support accounts, fake wallet apps, fake exchange websites. Always verify you are on the correct URL and using the official app.  
* **Urgency**: Scams often create time pressure to prevent you from thinking carefully. Slow down whenever anyone is pushing you to act quickly.

### **Start Small**

If you are new to Bitcoin, start with an amount you are comfortable losing entirely while you learn. Make a few transactions. Practice sending and receiving. Recover your wallet using your seed phrase on a second device. Build familiarity before you commit significant savings.

### **Use Open-Source Wallets**

As covered in Module 3, open-source wallet code can be audited by anyone. Closed-source wallets ask you to trust the developer blindly. In a system built on verifiability, this is an unnecessary risk.

## **Finding Bitcoin in Your Community**

Bitcoin's utility grows as more people around you use it. Finding local merchants, communities, and resources makes participation easier and supports the broader ecosystem.

**BTCMap.org** is an open-source, community-maintained map of merchants and businesses that accept bitcoin worldwide. Search by your city or region to find cafés, shops, service providers, and more that will take bitcoin as payment.

**Circular economies** are communities of people who actively buy from and sell to each other in bitcoin — keeping value circulating within the community rather than converting in and out of fiat at every step. Bitcoin Beach in El Zonte, El Salvador was the first community to demonstrate this model at scale. Today hundreds of circular economies exist around the world, many powered by the Lightning Network's low fees and instant settlement.

**Local Bitcoin communities** often organize meetups, study groups, and events where you can ask questions, meet people already using Bitcoin daily, and find recommendations specific to your region. BTCMap also lists many of these communities alongside merchant listings.
