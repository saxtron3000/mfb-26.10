# 1. Learning Objectives Status

The principal document identifies weak learning objectives as a core weakness. It states that objectives are not always precise or measurable and that some sections lack clear statements describing what learners should know or be able to do.

Therefore, this document should be treated as a working framework, not a final approved set of objectives.

---

# 2. Objective Design Principles

Learning objectives should be:

- precise;
- measurable;
- aligned to competencies;
- aligned to activities;
- aligned to assessment evidence;
- suitable for educator delivery;
- realistic for beginners;
- clear about essential vs optional vs advanced expectations;
- practical where skill acquisition is intended;
- supportive of critical thinking rather than passive memorization.

---

# 3. Program-Level Learning Aim

Learners should develop the knowledge, reasoning, confidence, and practical orientation needed to understand Bitcoin from first principles, discuss money and Bitcoin critically, and take informed next steps in real-world Bitcoin usage.

This aim must be refined into measurable objectives before final curriculum and certification design.

---

# 4. Learning Domains

## 4.1 Knowledge

Learners should know key concepts related to:

- money;
- value;
- scarcity;
- trade;
- functions of money;
- properties of money;
- fiat systems;
- cryptocurrency;
- stablecoins;
- CBDCs;
- Bitcoin;
- Bitcoin history and design;
- basic technical foundations;
- cryptography and security;
- Bitcoin as money;
- Bitcoin as asset;
- use cases and adoption;
- risks, criticisms, and limitations.

## 4.2 Understanding

Learners should understand:

- why money exists;
- how monetary systems shape behavior;
- how different money systems compare;
- why Bitcoin matters to different people and communities;
- how practical Bitcoin use differs from theoretical ideal use;
- why self-custody matters and what barriers learners may face;
- the difference between on-chain and Lightning use;
- how economic, technical, social, and philosophical themes interact.

## 4.3 Skills

Learners should be able to:

- explain key ideas in their own words;
- compare monetary systems;
- analyze real-world scenarios using economic reasoning;
- discuss Bitcoin using evidence and reasoning;
- identify trade-offs;
- participate in structured dialogue;
- reflect on personal and community relevance;
- complete practical Bitcoin learning tasks where included;
- evaluate claims, criticisms, benefits, and limitations.

## 4.4 Attitudes / Dispositions

Learners should develop:

- agency;
- curiosity;
- confidence;
- independent reasoning;
- willingness to ask questions;
- ability to learn socially;
- respect for local context;
- caution around security and self-custody;
- seriousness about money, technology, and responsibility.

# 5. Draft Program-Level Objectives

By the end of the redesigned Bitcoin Diploma, learners should be able to:

1. Define foundational monetary concepts using clear language.
2. Explain why money exists and what problems it helps solve.
3. Identify the functions and properties of money.
4. Compare different monetary systems and instruments.
5. Explain the basic purpose of Bitcoin from first principles.
6. Distinguish practical Bitcoin usage from advanced technical theory.
7. Analyze the trade-offs involved in Bitcoin use, custody, and payment methods.
8. Discuss Bitcoin’s benefits, risks, criticisms, and limitations using reasoned arguments.
9. Apply economic reasoning to real-world examples.
10. Demonstrate beginner-appropriate practical competence where practical activities are included.
11. Participate in structured discussions and collaborative learning.
12. Reflect on how Bitcoin education connects to personal agency, community context, and local conditions.

# 6. Draft Competency Areas

## 6.1 Monetary Foundations
Learners can explain what money is, why it exists, and what makes money useful.

### Possible Evidence
- Written explanation.
- Discussion contribution.
- Comparison table.
- Scenario analysis.

## 6.2 Economic Reasoning
Learners can use concepts such as scarcity, value, incentives, and trade-offs to analyze real situations.

### Possible Evidence
- Case study.
- Reflection.
- Group discussion output.
- Short written response.

## 6.3 Bitcoin Fundamentals
Learners can explain Bitcoin’s basic purpose, design rationale, and role as a monetary technology.

### Possible Evidence
- Concept map.
- Short explanation.
- Peer teaching task.
- Quiz or written response.

## 6.4 Practical Bitcoin Orientation
Learners can identify realistic beginner actions and safety considerations when approaching Bitcoin use.

### Possible Evidence
- Practical scenario.
- Wallet/custody decision reflection.
- Lightning vs on-chain comparison.
- Risk checklist.

## 6.5 Critical Evaluation
Learners can assess claims about Bitcoin, including benefits, criticisms, and limitations.

### Possible Evidence
- Debate.
- Written argument.
- Source evaluation task.
- Presentation.

## 6.6 Social Learning and Dialogue
Learners can participate in structured discussion, listen to others, explain their reasoning, and revise ideas.

### Possible Evidence
- Think-pair-share output.
- Peer feedback.
- Discussion notes.
- Reflection.

## 6.7 Local Application
Learners can connect Bitcoin concepts to their local economic, social, and cultural context.

### Possible Evidence
- Local case study.
- Community problem analysis.
- Adapted example.
- Reflection.

# 7. Bloom’s Taxonomy Framework

Supporting documents propose objectives across the following levels.

## Remember
- Define key concepts such as money, value, scarcity, and Bitcoin.

## Understand
- Explain functions of money and types of currency systems.

## Apply
- Use economic reasoning to analyze real-world scenarios.

## Analyze
- Compare different monetary systems, including fiat, cryptocurrency, and barter.

## Evaluate
- Assess criticisms, benefits, risks, and limitations of Bitcoin.

## Create
- Design alternative monetary systems or solutions to economic problems.

### Caution
The “Create” level may be ambitious for beginner learners and should be confirmed before inclusion as a required outcome.

# 8. Assessment Alignment

## 8.1 Formative Assessment

Possible formative evidence:

- classroom discussions;
- think-pair-share outputs;
- reflection journals;
- exit tickets;
- activity completion;
- peer feedback;
- concept checks;
- misconception checks.

## 8.2 Summative Assessment

Possible summative evidence:

- project;
- case study analysis;
- presentation;
- podcast;
- written explanation;
- written exam;
- essay;
- practical demonstration.

## 8.3 Certification Alignment

Certification should be competency-based and aligned to learning outcomes.

### Certification Questions
- What evidence proves competence?
- Who verifies competence in decentralized teaching environments?
- What is the difference between participation, completion, and certification?
- What must be standardized across communities?
- What can be adapted locally?
- What assessment evidence works for in-person, online, self-paced, and hybrid delivery?

# 9. Objective Gaps to Resolve

The following gaps come directly from the review concerns:

- Objectives are not yet consistently measurable.
- Competencies are not yet fully mapped to lessons.
- Assessments are not yet fully mapped to competencies.
- Practical skills are not yet consistently structured through PPP.
- Beginner technical prerequisites need clarification.
- Essential vs optional vs advanced outcomes need definition.
- Online and self-paced checkpoint mechanisms need stronger design.
- Certification evidence needs to be defined before certification structure is finalized.
