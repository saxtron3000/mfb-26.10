## Welcome To The Bitcoin Diploma

### About Us

My First Bitcoin is a nonprofit education organization founded by John Dennehy in El Salvador in 2021. We publish free and open-source Bitcoin education for learners and educators around the world.

The project began with small in-person classes in El Salvador in 2021. The 90-minute introduction moved from what money is to how Bitcoin works, then gave the learner practical experience sending and receiving sats with a Lightning wallet. Within two months, the project was teaching hundreds of learners each month. My First Bitcoin's [public history](https://myfirstbitcoin.org/about) records the project's milestones and organizational accountability.

In 2022, the first Bitcoin Diploma cohort began in one public school in San Marcos, El Salvador. The pilot developed into an open-source curriculum that independent educators could translate, adapt, and teach. It later spread through the [My First Bitcoin Education Network](https://myfirstbitcoin.org/about/network) to communities around the world.

Our work is independent, community-driven, and Bitcoin-only. We focus on financial literacy, careful thinking, and practical safety—not trading, speculation, or promoting other tokens. We do not ask you to accept a belief. Instead, we provide concepts, questions, and practical knowledge so you can form your own conclusions.

### Our mission: independent, open-source Bitcoin education

Our mission is to empower people through independent, impartial, and open-source Bitcoin education.

Independent education is not controlled by a government, company, or financial service. Impartial education explains evidence and trade-offs without requiring a learner to reach a predetermined conclusion. Open-source education can be read, shared, reviewed, translated, and improved by anyone under its license.

This approach matters because money affects every person. People should be able to study the systems they use without needing permission. They should also be able to inspect the educational material, question its claims, and help improve it.

### What we stand for: accessibility, critical thinking, community, and self-sovereignty

**Accessibility** means that cost, location, language, or limited technology should not prevent learning. The essential path in this book can be used offline. Local examples may change, but the main learning outcomes remain the same.

**Curiosity** means being willing to ask why, admit what you do not know, and investigate an unfamiliar answer before accepting or rejecting it.

**Critical thinking** means asking clear questions, checking evidence, and comparing trade-offs. Bitcoin makes strong claims about money and ownership. These claims deserve careful study. During this course, distinguish facts from opinions and possibilities from guarantees.

**Community** means that education becomes more useful when people discuss ideas and connect them to local life. Your educator and classmates may have experiences that no global book can contain. Listen carefully, ask questions, and explain your reasoning with respect.

**Self-sovereignty** means having meaningful control over your own choices and resources. With Bitcoin, greater control can reduce dependence on institutions. It also creates greater personal responsibility. Control without knowledge can be dangerous, so practical safety is a central part of this Diploma.

### Why this Diploma exists

Most people use money before they learn how money works. They may earn it, save it, borrow it, and spend it without studying who issues it, how its supply changes, or why its purchasing power changes.

Bitcoin introduces a different system. It has no central issuer, it follows public rules, and it allows direct digital ownership. These ideas are difficult to understand without first examining money and the problems that can arise in modern money systems.

This Diploma therefore follows a simple path:

1. Understand the problem Bitcoin was designed to address.
2. Understand Bitcoin's main response.
3. Understand the responsibility of holding and using bitcoin.
4. Explore how Bitcoin may function as money in your community.

The aim is not to make you an expert in eight hours. The aim is to give you a reliable foundation, help you avoid common beginner mistakes, and prepare you to continue learning.

Treat this Diploma as the beginning of a longer learning process. By the end, you should know enough to ask better questions and to recognize which topics require more study.

You can use the course to:

## How This Diploma Works

### What learners will be able to explain and demonstrate by the end

After completing this Core Diploma, you should be able to:

- explain what money is and why people use it;
- identify the functions and useful properties of money;
- describe the path from barter to modern money;
- explain inflation, purchasing power, and fractional reserve banking through essential concepts;
- explain why Bitcoin was created and how its rules differ from those of modern money systems;
- describe decentralization, finite supply, peer-to-peer transfer, and verification;
- identify the roles of users, nodes, and miners;
- explain wallets, addresses, private keys, seed phrases, and custody;
- compare custodial and non-custodial use, and hot and cold wallets;

- discuss how Bitcoin may function as money in a local community.

These outcomes are a foundation, not proof of expert skill. Completing the Diploma does not mean you are ready to manage large amounts of bitcoin, advise other people, or solve advanced technical problems without support.

### Safety, responsibility, and no financial advice

This Diploma is for educational purposes only. It does not provide financial, investment, legal, or tax advice. It does not promise that bitcoin will increase in price. It does not recommend that you buy, sell, borrow against, or hold any amount of bitcoin.

The Diploma is Bitcoin-only. Other tokens, trading products, and broader cryptocurrency promotion are outside its scope. This boundary defines the subject of the course; it is not a guarantee about Bitcoin or a recommendation to acquire it.

Bitcoin's market price can change quickly. Transactions can be difficult or impossible to reverse. A lost seed phrase, exposed private key, dishonest custodian, software error, or scam can cause permanent loss.

Use three rules throughout the course:

1. **Learn first.** Do not act only because of excitement, fear, or pressure.
2. **Start small.** Test unfamiliar tools and processes with an amount you can afford to lose.
3. **Don't Trust, Verify.** Check addresses, amounts, fees, sources, and people before you approve an action.

Responsibility also includes knowing when to ask for help. Seek help before moving meaningful funds, but never reveal a seed phrase or private key to the person helping you.

## 1. Problem / Broken Money

**Learner promise:** You will understand the money problems Bitcoin responds to.

### Opening questions about money

**Opening reflection | Suggested time: 10 minutes | Individual writing, then cohort discussion**

Money is familiar, but it is not simple. Before the course discussion begins, write a short answer to each question.

1. Why do people need money?
2. What makes something money?
3. Who can create or control the money you use?
4. Why does the money you use have value?
5. Does your money buy the same amount today as it did in the past?

Keep your answers. Return to them at the end of the Diploma. This course may strengthen some of your views and change others.

As you discuss these questions, notice the difference between a fact and a preference. “This note is accepted for taxes” is a fact that can be checked. “This is the best form of money” is a conclusion that needs criteria and evidence.

### Why money exists

People have different skills, resources, needs, and plans. Exchange allows them to specialize. A farmer can grow food, a builder can build homes, and a nurse can provide care. Each person can trade the value of their work for the work of others.

Direct exchange is often difficult. The person who has what you need may not want what you offer. Money solves this coordination problem. It is an asset that people accept because they expect other people to accept it later.

Money helps strangers cooperate across distance and time. It allows a worker to receive value today and use it next week or next year. It also creates a common way to record prices, debts, income, costs, and savings.

Money is not the food, home, time, or skill that people value. It is a tool for measuring and exchanging claims on those things. Its usefulness depends on trust, rules, social acceptance, and its monetary properties.

**Spot question:** Why did money come into existence? Explain the exchange or coordination problem it helps people solve.

### Functions and properties of money

Money usually performs three main functions.

**Medium of exchange:** People accept it in payment for goods and services. This reduces the need for direct barter.

**Unit of account:** Prices are expressed in a shared unit. This allows people to compare the cost of different goods and keep financial records.

**Store of value:** Value can be moved from the present into the future. No money stores value perfectly, but some forms lose purchasing power more slowly than others.

An item can perform one function better than another. A national currency may be widely accepted for daily payments while another asset may be chosen for long-term saving.

Useful money also tends to have several properties:

| Property | Question to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Durability | Can it remain usable over time? |
| Portability | Can people move or transfer it without excessive difficulty? |
| Divisibility | Can it represent both small and large amounts? |
| Fungibility | Are equal units normally interchangeable? |
| Scarcity | Is its supply difficult to increase without limit? |
| Acceptability | Will other people receive it in exchange? |
| Verifiability | Can people check that it is genuine? |

No form of money scores perfectly. Gold is durable and scarce but difficult to divide and transport for small payments. Paper cash is portable and accepted locally, but it can be damaged and its supply can expand. The best comparison depends on the purpose, place, and time.

**Think critically:** Name a time when you chose one type of money or asset over another. What was the purpose? Compare the properties of the options you could have used. Which properties mattered most to you, and why?

### Barter and the double coincidence of wants

**Barter** is the direct exchange of one good or service for another. It can work well in a small exchange. It becomes difficult when many people and products are involved.

Imagine that Amina has rice and wants shoes. Luis has shoes but wants cooking oil, not rice. Mei has cooking oil and wants repair work. Amina may need several exchanges before she can obtain the shoes. Each exchange takes time and requires agreement about value.

For barter to work directly, each person must want what the other person offers at the same time and in suitable amounts. Economists call this the **double coincidence of wants**.

Barter also creates other problems:

- some goods cannot be divided without destroying their value;
- some goods spoil before they can be exchanged;
- there is no common unit for comparing prices;
- saving large amounts can be difficult;
- delayed payment and accounting become complex.

A commonly accepted money separates selling from buying. Amina can sell rice to one person for money and later use that money to buy shoes from someone else.

### Scarcity, value, and exchange

Resources are limited, while human wants are not. **Scarcity** means that choosing one use of a resource prevents another use. Time, land, energy, good health, tools, and money are all scarce.

The value of a good is not contained inside the object. People value goods according to their needs, preferences, information, and circumstances. Clean water may be inexpensive near a safe public source and extremely valuable during a shortage.

Exchange happens when both sides prefer what they receive to what they give up. They do not need to agree that the two items have the same personal value. They only need to agree to the terms.

Three ideas help explain everyday choices:

- **Opportunity cost** is the best alternative you give up when you make a choice. Money spent on transport cannot also be spent on food.
- **Time preference** describes how strongly a person prefers value now instead of later. Urgent needs can create a high preference for the present. Saving for a future goal shows a greater willingness to wait.
- **Trade-off** means gaining one benefit while accepting a cost or giving up another benefit.

Scarcity alone does not guarantee value. An item may be rare and still have no demand. A useful monetary asset combines limited supply with properties and demand that make people willing to hold or exchange it.

### Beginner monetary history from barter to digital money

Money did not develop in one identical sequence everywhere. Different societies used different goods, records, and institutions. However, a broad pattern helps explain modern money.

> “Money … is by no means the result of an agreement … but is the unintended product of historical development.”
>
> — Carl Menger, “On the Origin of Money” (1892)

**Direct exchange and local records:** People used barter, gifts, obligations, and written or remembered debts. These systems depended strongly on local relationships.

**Commodity money:** Communities selected goods that were widely valued and easier to trade. Salt, beads, shells, livestock, copper, silver, and gold have served monetary roles in different places. A commodity has a use or value beyond an issuer's promise.

**Standard coins:** Authorities measured and stamped metal into standard units. Coins improved recognition and accounting. Authorities could also reduce the precious-metal content of these coins while keeping the same stated value. This is called debasement.

**Representative paper:** Transporting and protecting metal was difficult. Depositories and banks issued paper claims that could be redeemed for a stated amount of metal. The paper was easier to move, but users had to trust the issuer to keep enough metal and honor redemption. Under different versions of a **gold standard**, a currency unit was legally connected to a stated amount of gold. The rules varied by place and period, and governments or banks sometimes suspended or ended convertibility.

**Bank and government money:** Banks issued more claims and credit. Governments created central banks and national currency systems. Money became increasingly based on institutional records and promises rather than direct possession of a commodity by the population. In many countries, national currency later became no longer redeemable for a physical asset such as gold. This separation from commodity backing is a defining feature of today’s **fiat money** (see below).

**Digital money:** Much of today's money moves as digital records through banks and payment services. A card or phone does not create a new kind of money by itself. It is usually an interface to a bank deposit or another claim denominated in the national currency.

This history shows repeated trade-offs. Convenience increased, but users often became more dependent on monetary policy, issuers, banks, and payment intermediaries.

### Fiat money

**Fiat money** is currency issued under government authority, usually through a central bank, that is not redeemable for a fixed amount of a commodity such as gold. The word fiat is Latin for “let it be done,” often understood as “by decree.” A country’s laws typically designate its currency as legal tender and require it for the payment of taxes, although the exact rules for accepting payments and settling debts vary by jurisdiction.

Fiat money works because people, businesses, banks, and governments accept it. Network effects reinforce this acceptance: people want the currency because other people use it and prices are stated in it.

The fiat system has several main participants:

| Participant | Core role |
| --- | --- |
| Central bank | Issues base money, manages monetary policy, supports payment settlement, and may act as lender of last resort. |
| Commercial bank | Holds customer deposits, provides payments, and creates deposit money when it issues loans. |
| Government | Collects taxes, spends, borrows, creates laws, and usually defines the national unit of account. |
| Citizens and businesses | Earn, save, borrow, invest, pay taxes, set prices, and exchange goods and services. |

**Local scenario:** Choose one ordinary payment in your community, such as buying transport, food, or phone credit; receiving wages; or paying a bill. Use a realistic invented scenario if you prefer. In three or four sentences, explain:

1. what is being paid for and which currency is used as the unit of account;
2. why the seller or recipient is willing to accept that currency; and
3. which institutions, records, or rules people rely on to complete the payment.

Finish by naming one way the payment would become harder if that shared acceptance or trust broke down.

Fiat systems can provide convenient payments, flexible credit, and tools for responding to financial crises. The same flexibility creates **risk**. A small number of institutions can change the supply and cost of money, restrict access, or make decisions that reduce purchasing power.

Fiat money requires layers of **trust**. Users trust banks to keep accurate records, payment services to process transfers, central banks to manage the currency, and governments to maintain lawful institutions. These systems can work for long periods, but their rules are not fixed and can be open to neglect or abuse.

### Fractional reserve banking

When you deposit cash in a bank, the bank records a liability to you: it owes you the account balance. The bank does not normally keep every unit of every deposit as physical cash. It holds liquid reserves and uses its balance sheet to make loans and other investments.

When a commercial bank approves a loan, it usually creates a new deposit in the borrower's account. This expands the amount of bank deposit money. When the principal is repaid, that deposit money is reduced. Banks are limited by capital rules, liquidity needs, credit risk, regulation, the cost of funding, and demand from qualified borrowers.

Banks generally seek profit by earning interest and fees on loans and investments while paying their operating, funding, and regulatory costs and absorbing losses when borrowers do not repay. These activities and constraints differ by bank and jurisdiction.

**Fractional reserve banking** is the broad idea that banks keep only a fraction of their deposit obligations available as immediately accessible reserves. Exact rules differ between countries and can change over time.

This creates two important risks:

- **Liquidity risk:** A bank may own long-term loans that are expected to be repaid but still lack enough cash for many withdrawals today.
- **Solvency risk:** Failed loans or falling asset values may leave the bank's total assets worth less than what it owes.

A **bank run** happens when many depositors try to withdraw at the same time because they fear the bank may fail. Deposit insurance and central-bank lending can reduce panic, but insurance eligibility and coverage limits vary by jurisdiction. These protections depend on institutions and rules, and they can move some losses or risks from an individual bank to the wider system.

Regulation and oversight can reduce risk, but they cannot prevent every case of fraud, poor management, political abuse, or simple error. These failures are possibilities to evaluate, not outcomes of every banking system.

The central lesson is not that every bank is about to fail. It is that a bank balance is the bank's promise to pay you. It is not the same as holding the settlement asset directly.

### Inflation and purchasing power

**Price inflation** is a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services. If your income and savings do not rise as quickly as prices, your money buys less.

**Purchasing power** is the amount of goods and services a unit of money can buy. If a basket of basic goods rises from 100 units of currency to 110, the same 100 units no longer buy the full basket. Its purchasing power in relation to that basket has fallen.

Inflation can have several causes. These include an expansion of money and credit, supply disruptions, changes in demand, currency weakness, taxes, regulation, and changes in production costs. The causes and effects are often debated. Avoid explanations that assign every price change to one factor.

Inflation does not affect everyone equally. A person who holds mainly cash may lose purchasing power. A person who owns an asset that rises in price may be partly protected. Borrowers may repay fixed debts with money that buys less, while lenders may receive less real value than expected. Wages and prices also adjust at different speeds.

The rate of inflation is an average. Your personal experience may differ because your spending is not identical to the measured basket. Food, housing, transport, education, and health costs may change at different rates.

**Purchasing-power check:** Choose one ordinary item in your community. Record its price today. Ask what it cost several years ago, then check a reliable record if possible. Did income change at the same rate? What other factors may explain the change?

Then compare your item with an official inflation measure. Which goods and services are in that measure's basket? Has the basket or method changed over time, and does the source explain why? A change is not automatically manipulation, but it can affect comparisons and should be documented transparently.

### Why the US dollar is a world reserve currency

A **reserve currency** is a currency that central banks and governments hold and use for international payments. Before the First World War, the British pound played this role most strongly. The war weakened European currencies and financial centres, while the United States supplied goods and accumulated gold. This increased international use of the dollar. After the Second World War, the United States had a large economy and much of its financial capacity intact. The 1944 Bretton Woods Agreement then organized a postwar system in which many currencies were linked to the dollar and the dollar was officially linked to gold, placing the dollar at the centre of international payments.

In 1971, President Nixon ended the official exchange of dollars for gold by foreign governments and central banks, helping bring the Bretton Woods system to an end. The dollar nevertheless remained widely used because of its established role in trade and finance, the size of the U.S. economy, and the depth of U.S. financial markets. It remains the leading reserve currency today, although central banks also hold other currencies and its share can change over time.

### Why people look for alternatives (Precious Metals, Real Estate, Stocks)

People often seek alternatives when they believe cash will lose purchasing power. Common choices include precious metals, real estate, and stocks. Each has benefits and risks.

| Alternative | Why people may choose it | Important trade-offs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Precious metals | Long history, physical possession, limited supply | Storage, verification, transport, security, and difficult small payments |
| Real estate | Useful physical asset, possible rent, limited desirable locations | High entry cost, maintenance, taxes, legal dependence, and low portability |
| Stocks | Ownership in productive businesses, possible income and growth | Business risk, market volatility, custody through institutions, and need for financial knowledge |

These assets are not identical to money. A home may store value but is not practical for buying food. A share may be easy to sell in one country but unavailable to someone without a brokerage account. Gold may cross long periods of history but be difficult to verify or divide during a small exchange.

People may also choose foreign currency, government bonds, private businesses, land, tools, education, or other assets. Every choice includes opportunity cost and risk. There is no asset that guarantees purchasing power in every period and situation.

**Note:** Tax treatment can also shape these decisions. Rules for income, gains, property, and transactions can change the cost or benefit of holding, selling, or using an asset. Tax rules vary by jurisdiction and can change over time; this course does not provide tax advice.

Bitcoin emerged as another proposed alternative: a scarce digital asset that can be held and transferred without a central issuer. To evaluate that claim, we must study why Bitcoin was created and how its rules work.

### Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs)

A **central bank digital currency**, or **CBDC**, is a digital form of fiat money issued as a liability of a central bank. It is different from physical cash and from a normal commercial-bank deposit, although the user experience may look similar.

CBDC designs differ. Some are intended mainly for financial institutions. Others are proposed for public use. Depending on the design, a CBDC may allow faster settlement, wider access, or new payment features. It may also give institutions greater ability to monitor transactions, apply rules, limit use, or connect identity directly to payments.

A CBDC does not create a fixed monetary supply or remove central control. It changes the technology and distribution of fiat money. The central bank remains the issuer and can still apply monetary policy.

Digital does not automatically mean decentralized, scarce, private, or open to independent verification. A CBDC may use digital technology, but it is still fiat money if its issuer, supply rules, access rules, and payment permissions remain centrally managed.

When discussing a CBDC, ask:

- Who issues the money?
- Who can hold or use it?
- What information is collected?
- Can a payment be blocked, limited, or reversed?
- Can the rules change?
- Can ordinary users independently verify the rules?
- What happens without electricity or internet access?
- What legal protections exist?

Do not assume that every proposed CBDC has the same design. Use current, local, and reliable information.

### Review: how can money break?

Pause and consider the money you use: what makes it useful in daily life, and which of its rules depend on institutions you must trust? Barter is difficult because matching needs is inefficient, while fiat money makes exchange easier but can involve changing supply, restricted access, and declining purchasing power. In short, people look for monetary alternatives when they want different trade-offs around supply, control, access, and trust.

#### Key takeaways

- Money helps people exchange, compare prices, record value, and move value across time.
- The main functions of money are a medium of exchange, a unit of account, and a store of value.
- Monetary properties such as durability, portability, divisibility, scarcity, acceptability, and verifiability help us compare different forms of money.
- Barter is difficult at scale because both people must want what the other offers at the same time and in suitable amounts.
- Money has developed in different ways across societies, often improving convenience while creating new trade-offs in trust, control, and access.
- Fiat money can support convenient payments and flexible credit, but its supply and rules depend on institutions.
- Inflation, fractional-reserve banking, and CBDC design can affect purchasing power, access, privacy, and the need to trust institutions.
- Some people look to alternatives such as precious metals when they want different trade-offs around purchasing power, supply, or control; every alternative has limits and risks.

## 2. Solution / Bitcoin

**Learner promise:** You will understand why Bitcoin was created and what makes it different.

### Why Bitcoin was created

Digital payments existed before Bitcoin. People could send money through banks, card networks, and online payment companies. These systems made commerce easier, but each depended on a trusted organization to keep the ledger, approve accounts, settle transfers, and resolve disputes.

This dependence creates power and risk. An intermediary can delay a payment, charge a fee, collect  and misuse personal data, restrict an account, reverse a transfer, or fail. The issuer of the currency can also change its supply.

For many years, computer scientists tried to create digital money that did not need a central issuer or payment company. The challenge was difficult. Digital information is easy to copy. If a digital coin could be copied and spent twice, it could not function as money. If one company prevented copying, the company became the point of control and failure.

Bitcoin was created to solve this problem with a peer-to-peer network. Its design allows participants to agree on one transaction history without a central ledger keeper. It combines a public ledger, digital signatures, a network of validating nodes, proof of work, and economic incentives.

Bitcoin does not remove trust from every part of life. It reduces the need to trust a central authority for the issuance and settlement of its native money.

In this book, **Bitcoin** with a capital B usually means the network, protocol, or wider system. **bitcoin** with a lowercase b means the monetary unit or asset. A wallet or exchange is a service that may connect to Bitcoin; it is not the Bitcoin network itself.

### The search for digital and decentralized money

The growth of personal computers and the internet created new opportunities for communication. It also created new forms of surveillance and control. In the late twentieth century, a group of programmers, mathematicians, and activists known as **Cypherpunks** explored how cryptography could protect privacy and individual freedom in digital systems.

**Cryptography** is the use of mathematical methods to protect information and prove that an action is authentic. Encryption can keep a message private. **Digital signatures** can prove that a message or instruction came from the holder of a secret key without revealing that key.

The Cypherpunks understood that private communication was not enough. People also needed a way to exchange value online. If every digital payment required a bank or company, that intermediary could observe, permit, or prevent the exchange.

Their goal was not simply digital money. Bank deposits were already digital. The harder goal was **decentralized digital money**: a system with no single issuer, server, or administrator that participants had to trust.

Bitcoin was built upon decades of work by many people. Its design joined earlier ideas in a new way and provided incentives for an open network to keep operating.

### Early digital-money attempts and the double-spend problem

Bitcoin was not the first attempt at digital money. It was built on four decades of experimentation, drawing on ideas from many earlier projects that helped shape its successful design.

**DigiCash** used cryptography to support private digital payments. It depended on a central company and banking relationships. When the company failed, the system could not continue as an independent money network.

**Hashcash** used proof of work to make unwanted email costly. A sender's computer had to perform work before sending a message. The idea showed how computational cost could discourage abuse.

**Bit Gold** described a proposed system in which computational work could create scarce digital records. It was not launched as a complete decentralized money system, but it developed important ideas about digital scarcity and proof.

**B-money** and **reusable proof of work** explored ways for participants to create or transfer scarce digital value without relying only on one central database. They were incomplete, but they helped clarify what a working system would need.

**BitTorrent** was not money. It showed that a peer-to-peer network could let many independent computers share information without one central server controlling the whole system.

Together, these experiments showed useful pieces of the puzzle. They also clarified the remaining problem.

The central problem was **double spending**. A paper note cannot be given to two people at the same time. However, digital data can be copied perfectly. A dishonest user could try to send the same digital unit to two recipients.

Centralized systems prevent this by letting one trusted database decide which payment came first. On 31 October 2008, Satoshi Nakamoto's whitepaper, *Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System*, explained a different approach: a public system that could establish a shared payment history without a bank, company, or other central authority.

The whitepaper brought together the features needed for that approach:

1. **A peer-to-peer network:** transactions are announced directly to a network of participants.
2. **Digital signatures:** only the holder of the relevant private key can authorize a spend.
3. **Blocks and mining:** miners collect transactions into blocks and use proof of work to compete to add the next block.
4. **Independent verification:** nodes check transactions and blocks against Bitcoin's rules.
5. **A shared history with accumulated proof of work:** participants recognize the valid chain of transactions with the most accumulated work as the shared record.

This module explains these concepts in more detail. Together, they gave a practical answer to the double-spend problem: the network can reject a second attempt to spend the same bitcoin without relying on a central authority. Bitcoin offered the missing combination that decades of digital-money experiments had been seeking.

### Digital scarcity and credible scarcity

Digital information is normally easy to copy. Digital scarcity means that the system can distinguish a valid unit from an invalid copy and can prevent the same unit from being spent twice.

Bitcoin creates digital scarcity by combining signatures, a public transaction history, proof of work, and independent rule verification. A bitcoin can be transferred, but the network rejects an attempt to spend the same bitcoin again.

Scarcity is more credible when the rules are public, users can verify them independently, and no single issuer can quietly change the supply for everyone. This is why Bitcoin's supply limit must be studied together with decentralization and node verification.

### Decentralization

A centralized system has a controlling point. A bank controls its customer ledger. A payment company controls its network. A government controls the issuance rules of its currency.

A **decentralized** system distributes roles and decision enforcement among many independent participants. Bitcoin has no central office that can create accounts, change balances, or approve membership.

Bitcoin's decentralization comes from several features:

- anyone can download and inspect the open-source software;
- users can transact without opening an account with the Bitcoin network;
- independent nodes check transactions and blocks;
- miners compete to propose blocks;
- no miner can make an invalid block acceptable to honest nodes;
- changes to the rules require voluntary adoption by participants;
- the ledger is copied across many computers and locations.

Decentralization is not the same as equal influence. Mining equipment, technical knowledge, money, internet access, and software development are not distributed equally. Large service providers can become points of dependence for their own users. Bitcoin's claim is narrower: no single participant has the authority to unilaterally change the ledger or monetary rules for everyone.

Decentralization also creates costs. Coordination can be slower. Users must verify information and take more responsibility. There is no central support desk for the Bitcoin network. These trade-offs are part of the design, not accidental defects that can be removed without changing the system.

### Digital signatures

Digital signatures are one of the key parts of Bitcoin's design described in the whitepaper. In Bitcoin, a wallet uses a private key to create a digital signature that authorizes a transaction. A wallet is usually an application that manages the keys and creates these signatures for the user. Only the holder of the required private key can authorize the spend; the network can verify the signature without learning the private key itself.

This allows a person to control bitcoin without asking a bank or other central operator for permission. It also means that control of the private key is critical: anyone who obtains it can usually authorize a spend, while a lost key may make the bitcoin irretrievable.

### Peer-to-peer transfer

**Peer-to-peer** means that participants interact through a network without requiring a central coordinator. A Bitcoin user can create and sign a transaction, then broadcast it to the network. The recipient does not need an account at the sender's bank because Bitcoin itself provides the common settlement system.

Peer-to-peer does not always mean that only two computers communicate directly. A transaction may pass through wallets, internet providers, and many network nodes. The important point is that no central Bitcoin operator must approve the sender or recipient.

Users can still choose intermediaries. An exchange may hold bitcoin for customers. A payment service may simplify an invoice. A hosted wallet may manage keys. These services can be convenient, but the user then depends on the service's rules and security.

Bitcoin makes direct ownership and transfer possible; it does not force every user to avoid every service. The custody choice determines who has practical control.

Peer-to-peer transfer can be useful when people are in different places or use different banking systems. It also creates responsibility. A valid transaction sent to the wrong address cannot usually be reversed by a central authority.

### Users, nodes, and miners

Bitcoin works because different participants perform different roles.

**Users** receive, hold, and send bitcoin. A user may control their own keys held in a wallet or use a custodian. Users give the network economic meaning by choosing whether to accept bitcoin and which rules or services they trust.

**Nodes** run Bitcoin software and independently verify the rules. A full node checks that transactions have valid signatures, that bitcoin have not already been spent, that blocks satisfy proof of work, and that new bitcoin follow the issuance schedule. Nodes share valid data with peers and reject invalid data. 

**Miners** gather pending transactions, build candidate blocks, and compete through proof of work. A successful miner broadcasts a block and may receive the allowed block subsidy and transaction fees. Miners help order transactions and make the ledger costly to rewrite.

**Developers** review, write, and propose software changes. They do not own Bitcoin's rules. A software change matters only if participants choose to run software that follows those rules.

These roles limit one another:

- a user cannot spend bitcoin without the required valid signature;
- a miner can propose a block, but nodes decide whether it follows the rules;
- a node verifies using its own rules, but consensus requires compatible rules across the network;
- developers can propose software, but participants choose whether to run it.

Some people perform several roles. A miner can run a node and also be a user. A beginner does not need to perform every role to understand the balance among them.

**Spot question:** Why does a node verify transactions? Name at least one rule that a node checks.

### Proof of work

**Proof of work** is the process miners use to compete for the right to propose the next block.

A miner repeatedly changes data in a candidate block and passes it through a mathematical hash function. The result is unpredictable. The miner is searching for a result below a target set by the network's difficulty rule. There is no shortcut; miners must perform many attempts.

When a miner finds a valid result, it broadcasts the block. Nodes can check the proof quickly, even though finding it required significant computation and energy. If the block includes a transaction, that transaction receives its first **confirmation**. Each valid block added after it adds another confirmation, making the transaction's place in the blockchain more costly to rewrite. This difference is important: work is costly to produce and easy to verify.

Proof of work serves several purposes:

- it gives independent miners a way to compete without a central scheduler;
- it makes the ordering of transactions costly to rewrite;
- it links network security to real-world equipment and energy;
- it supports a public method for choosing between competing valid histories: the chain with the most accumulated work.

Mining does not solve useful equations for an outside purpose. Its purpose is to secure the ordering of Bitcoin transactions. The network adjusts mining difficulty so that blocks continue to arrive about every ten minutes on average as computing power changes.

Proof of work involves a real energy cost. Supporters argue that this cost is what anchors Bitcoin's security and can create demand for flexible or otherwise unused energy. Critics question the amount and source of energy used. A responsible evaluation asks what energy is used, where it comes from, what security it provides, and what alternatives are being compared.

### Finite supply

Bitcoin's rules define a predictable issuance schedule and a maximum supply of 21 million bitcoin. New bitcoin enter circulation through the block subsidy paid to miners who produce valid blocks.

Every 210,000 blocks, the subsidy is cut in half. This event is called a **halving**. Because blocks are produced approximately every ten minutes on average, a halving occurs roughly every four years. The exact calendar date is not fixed.

| Halving year | Block height | Subsidy after halving |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 2012 | 210,000 | 25 bitcoin |
| 2016 | 420,000 | 12.5 bitcoin |
| 2020 | 630,000 | 6.25 bitcoin |
| 2024 | 840,000 | 3.125 bitcoin |

The subsidy began at 50 bitcoin per block. It falls by half in repeated steps until it becomes too small to continue. Miners also receive transaction fees. Over time, fees are expected to become a more important part of miner revenue.

The 21 million limit is enforced by nodes. If a miner creates more bitcoin than the rules allow, validating nodes reject the block. A software developer can write different code, but cannot force other people to run it.

Bitcoin's supply rule is credible because it is public, easy to verify with a node, and difficult for one party to change. It is not a physical law. It is a network rule that remains effective while participants continue to enforce it.

Many digital assets can claim a limited supply. The beginner question is whether the limit is credible in practice, not only whether it is written in a document or displayed on a website.

**Spot question:** What are the advantages of Bitcoin having a limited supply? What would be the effect of increasing the supply of Bitcoin on purchasing power? Who would reject an increase in supply?

Finite supply does not guarantee a rising market price. Price depends on demand as well as supply. It also does not mean every bitcoin is available for sale; some may be held long term or become inaccessible because keys were lost.

### Satoshis and unit bias

One bitcoin can be divided into **100,000,000 satoshis**. A satoshi, often shortened to **sat**, is the smallest unit recorded on Bitcoin's base layer.

| Unit | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| 1 bitcoin | 100,000,000 sats |
| 0.1 bitcoin | 10,000,000 sats |
| 0.01 bitcoin | 1,000,000 sats |
| 0.001 bitcoin | 100,000 sats |
| 0.00000001 bitcoin | 1 sat |

**You do not need to buy or use a whole bitcoin.** Bitcoin software can display an amount in bitcoin or sats. Many people use sats for everyday amounts because whole numbers are easier to read.

**Unit bias** is the mistaken feeling that a lower price per whole unit makes an asset cheaper or more accessible. The number of units is a design choice. Owning 100,000 sats and owning 0.001 bitcoin describe the same amount.

When comparing prices, focus on the share of the total supply, the amount of value involved, and the risks. Do not assume that an asset with many cheap-looking units offers better value than an asset divided into smaller units.

**Check your understanding:** How many sats are in 0.01 bitcoin? Why does the price of one whole bitcoin not prevent a person from using a smaller amount?

### Bitcoin as sound digital money

**Sound money** is money whose supply is difficult to expand and whose properties support reliable use across time. The term expresses a judgment based on criteria, so it should be examined rather than accepted as a label.

Bitcoin combines properties that did not previously exist together in one asset:

| Property | Plain-language explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Durability | The ledger is copied across many nodes. Access depends on preserving valid keys. |
| Portability | A signed transaction can transfer value through a communications network. |
| Divisibility | One bitcoin divides into 100,000,000 sats. |
| Fungibility | Equal amounts are spendable under the same protocol rules, although public transaction history can affect how some services treat particular funds. |
| Scarcity | Issuance follows public rules with a 21 million maximum. |
| Verifiability | A node can verify supply, transaction rules, and ownership proofs. |
| Acceptability | Acceptance is growing in some places but remains much lower than national currencies in many communities. |

Bitcoin can perform the three functions of money, but not equally in every place:

- As a **store of value**, its supply is predictable, but its market price is volatile.
- As a **medium of exchange**, it can move without a bank, but acceptance, fees, and payment tools vary.
- As a **unit of account**, amounts can be stated in bitcoin or sats, but most goods are still priced in fiat currency.

Bitcoin therefore presents both benefits and trade-offs.

**Potential benefits** include direct ownership, predictable supply, global transfer, open participation, transparent rules, and resistance to unilateral censorship.

**Trade-offs** include price volatility, personal security responsibility, irreversible errors, changing transaction fees, dependence on electricity and communications for digital transfer, public transaction records, and limited acceptance.

Whether you call Bitcoin sound money should follow from your criteria. Compare it with fiat money, precious metals, and other assets using the same standards.

**Local-value check:** Does bitcoin have value in your community? Separate three questions in your answer: What market price can be observed? Where, if anywhere, is it accepted? What personal preference or bias affects your judgment? Price, local usefulness, and personal approval are related but not identical.

A block explorer is a convenient interface to public data, not a primary authority over Bitcoin's rules.

**Satoshi Nakamoto** is the name used by Bitcoin's creator or creators. Satoshi communicated online, released the software as open source, and later stopped participating. Satoshi's real-world identity remains unknown.

No central company owns Bitcoin. The software and rules continued because other people chose to run, review, and develop them. This origin matters: the creator did not remain as a permanent leader who could issue more coins or approve transactions.

The Genesis Block and the 2008 financial crisis give Bitcoin important historical context. However, evaluate Bitcoin by its design and operation, not only by a story about its creator.

At the end of this timeline is the event that made Bitcoin possible: in October 2008, Satoshi Nakamoto announced the Bitcoin whitepaper. It proposed a practical solution to a problem that had challenged digital-money designers for decades—how to make online payments directly between people while preventing double spending without putting a central intermediary in control.

### Participant empowerment, risk, and responsibility

Bitcoin's public rules, peer-to-peer network, and digital signatures give participants meaningful roles. Users can choose to receive, hold, and send bitcoin; nodes can independently verify the rules; miners can propose blocks; and developers can propose software changes. No single role controls the network for everyone.

These properties can empower participants, but they also place more responsibility on them. Without a central authority to approve transactions, recover access, or reverse many errors, each person must understand the role and risks they accept.

For a person who chooses self-custody, control of the keys brings direct control of bitcoin. No company can freeze a correctly controlled on-chain balance. No company can restore a lost seed phrase either.

Self-custody is therefore not just a technical feature. It requires protecting backups, verifying addresses, understanding wallet behavior, and creating a recovery plan. The next block explains how to hold and use bitcoin carefully, including how to manage these risks and responsibilities.

Personal responsibility does not mean acting alone or refusing help. A cautious learner can use education, peer review, documented procedures, and small tests without giving anyone access to secret information.

## 3. Action / Self-Custody

### Wallets

A **Bitcoin wallet** is a tool for managing keys, creating receiving information, checking balances, and signing transactions. It may be an application, a dedicated hardware device, or a coordinated service.

The wallet does not store bitcoin inside the phone or device. Bitcoin remains recorded on the shared ledger. The wallet stores or controls the secret information that allows you to recieve, hold and spend bitcoin.

A wallet usually helps you:

- generate private keys securely;
- create addresses for receiving bitcoin;
- display transaction history and balances;
- build and sign outgoing transactions;
- estimate or select transaction fees;
- create a backup or recovery method;
- connect to the Bitcoin network directly or through a service.

Wallets make different trade-offs. Some prioritize simplicity. Others prioritize privacy, independent verification, or stronger security. Some support only on-chain Bitcoin. Some support Lightning. Some hold the keys for you.

Before choosing a wallet, compare the same areas for every option:

| Area | Question to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Custody | Who controls the private keys? |
| Source and review | Is the software open source, widely reviewed, and obtained from a verified source? |
| Recovery | Is recovery clearly documented, and can you test it safely? |
| Compatibility | Does it support the payment network, address or invoice types, device, and backup method you need? |
| Privacy | What personal, device, contact, or transaction information does it collect? |
| Verification | Does it connect to the provider's server or to a node you choose? |
| Continuity | Is the project maintained, and what happens if the company or project disappears? |

Do not choose a wallet only because it is popular, free, or recommended by a stranger. The right tool depends on the amount, purpose, device, experience, and local conditions.

If wallets are new to you, observe a complete educator demonstration before installing one. Practise with a prepared, empty training wallet or printed screens first. Use the official instructions for any tool, ask for help without revealing secret information, and stop if the process differs from what was demonstrated.

### Addresses

A Bitcoin **address** is information used to receive an on-chain payment. A wallet creates addresses from its keys and normally presents them as a line of letters and numbers and as a QR code.

An address is public receiving information that a sender's wallet uses to construct an on-chain payment. It identifies a payment destination; it is not a login credential or password. Sharing an address does not give someone permission to spend your bitcoin. It can, however, reduce privacy because transactions to and from addresses appear on the public ledger.

Good address practice includes:

- obtain the address from the receiving wallet;
- confirm that it is for Bitcoin and for the intended network;
- use copy-and-paste or a QR code to avoid manual typing;
- compare visible characters at the beginning and end after copying;
- for important payments, verify the complete address on a trusted display;
- use a new address for each on-chain receipt when the wallet provides one;
- send a small test first when the amount or process is unfamiliar.

Bitcoin addresses can begin with different characters because several valid formats exist. Do not decide that an address is safe only by its appearance.

A Lightning invoice is not the same as an on-chain address. Confirm the payment method before sending.

### Keys

Bitcoin ownership is enforced through cryptographic keys.

A **private key** is secret information that allows a wallet to create a valid digital signature. That signature authorizes the spending of bitcoin controlled by the corresponding key. Anyone who obtains the private key can usually spend those funds.

A **public key** is mathematically related to the private key. It can be used to verify a signature without revealing the private key. Addresses are created from public-key information through additional mathematical steps.

The essential rule is simple:

**Public receiving information can be shared. Private spending information must remain secret.**

A digital signature proves that the required private key authorized a transaction. It does not place the private key inside the transaction. Nodes verify the signature before accepting the spend.

Modern wallets manage many keys automatically. Beginners should not copy or handle individual private-key strings. To avoid loss of funds, protect the wallet's approved backup and follow its recovery procedure.

No legitimate educator, wallet company, exchange employee, technical-support worker, or Bitcoin developer needs your private key or seed phrase. Anyone who asks for it should be treated as a threat.

### Seed phrases

A **seed phrase**, also called a recovery phrase, is an ordered list of words that many wallets use to back up the information needed to recreate their keys. Common lengths are 12 or 24 words, although wallet designs differ.

The order and spelling of the words matter. If a person has the seed phrase, they can often restore the wallet on another compatible device and spend the funds. The seed phrase is therefore not only a backup. It is full access.

Protect a seed phrase with these rules:

- write it down privately and accurately during wallet creation;
- follow the wallet's official backup instructions;
- keep it offline;
- do not photograph, scan, email, print through an online service, or save it in cloud notes;
- do not type it into a website or ordinary computer form;
- do not send it through a message or password manager;
- do not speak it near a camera, microphone, or other people;
- store the backup where damage, loss, theft, or casual discovery are less likely;
- make sure a trusted recovery plan exists for serious illness or death, without exposing the words unnecessarily.

Some advanced wallets use additional passphrases, multiple keys, or other backup systems. These can improve security but can also create new ways to lose access. Do not add complexity you do not yet understand.

If a seed phrase may have been seen, copied, entered online, or stored on an untrusted device, treat it as compromised. Create a new wallet with a new seed using a trusted process, then move the funds after verifying the destination. Ask for guidance if needed, but never reveal the old or new phrase.

### Custodial and non-custodial use

**Custody** answers a practical question: who controls the keys required to spend the bitcoin?

In a **custodial** service, a company or another person controls the keys. Your displayed balance is a promise from the custodian. You usually sign in with an account and ask the service to process a withdrawal or payment.

In a **non-custodial** or **self-custodial** wallet, you control the keys. The wallet signs transactions under your authority. There may still be software, hardware, server, or network dependencies, but no custodian must approve a valid on-chain spend.

| Question | Custodial use | Self-custodial use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who holds the keys? | The service | The user |
| Who can restrict access? | The service may restrict it | Anyone with the keys can spend; no account operator is required |
| Who handles recovery? | Usually the service through an account process | The user through a backup process |
| Main convenience | Familiar login, support, and simple operation | Direct control and fewer custody intermediaries |
| Main risk | Failure, fraud, hacking, freezes, withdrawal limits, or changing rules | Lost or exposed keys, user error, unsafe backups, or poor tools |

Custody is not always obvious from the interface. An application may use the word “wallet” while holding all keys for its users. Check the documentation. If you cannot export or control the recovery method and need the company to approve a withdrawal, you are probably using a custodian.

Self-custody is a spectrum of responsibility, not a test of personal worth. A beginner may use a small custodial balance while learning and later move to self-custody. The important step is to understand the trust and risk involved.

### Hot and cold wallets

A **hot wallet** manages keys on a device connected to the internet, such as a phone or computer. It is convenient for regular payments. It also has a larger attack surface because malicious software, insecure updates, phishing, physical access, or device compromise may expose the wallet.

A **cold wallet** keeps the keys used for signing away from an internet-connected general-purpose device. A **hardware wallet** is a dedicated device designed to generate, protect, and use keys while keeping them isolated from the connected computer or phone.

| Use | Common strengths | Common risks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hot wallet | Fast access, simple payments, low cost | Online threats, phone loss, malicious applications, weaker isolation |
| Hardware or cold wallet | Stronger key isolation, suitable for less frequent access | Device cost, supply-chain risk, backup mistakes, complex recovery, loss or damage |
| Custodial account | Familiar login and support | Counterparty control and loss of direct ownership |

A hardware wallet does not remove the need for a seed backup. If the device breaks, recovery usually depends on that backup. If an attacker obtains the seed phrase, the hardware device cannot protect the funds.

For larger or long-term holdings, people may use advanced arrangements such as **multiple-signature**, or **multisignature**, wallets. These require a defined threshold of signatures from different keys—for example, two signatures from a set of three keys. In that example, one key alone cannot authorize a spend. Multisignature design and recovery are outside the Core Diploma. Greater complexity is useful only when the recovery plan is equally strong.

A practical approach is to separate purposes: a small amount in a convenient spending wallet and a more carefully secured amount in a wallet used less often. The actual amounts depend on personal circumstances. This is an educational security model, not financial advice.

### Wallet recovery and seed phrase testing

A backup is valuable only if it can recover the intended wallet. Testing should happen before meaningful funds depend on it.

There are two different checks:

**Backup verification** confirms that the words, order, and format were recorded correctly. Some hardware wallets provide an on-device backup-check feature. This is usually safer than entering the phrase into another device.

**Recovery practice** confirms that you understand the full process and can restore the expected wallet. A beginner should practice with a new, empty training wallet or a wallet containing only a very small test amount.

A safe beginner recovery process follows these principles:

1. Use official instructions for the exact wallet.
2. Confirm that the wallet and backup standard are compatible.
3. Practice in a private place without distractions or observers.
4. Prefer the wallet's built-in, trusted recovery or backup-check method.
5. Never enter a hardware-wallet seed phrase into a website, message, or ordinary computer application.
6. Confirm that the restored wallet shows the expected receiving information or test transaction history.
7. Clear or securely reset any temporary training device after the exercise.
8. Keep the permanent backup offline and private.

Do not test a backup by exposing the seed to an educator or classmate. They can explain the procedure without seeing the words.

**Spot question:** Using a prepared practice wallet, how would you recover access after the original device fails while keeping the recovery information private?

Recovery planning should answer:

- Where is the backup?
- What could damage or reveal it?
- Which wallet or standard created it?
- What instructions will a future user need?
- How can access be recovered if the original device fails?
- How can heirs recover funds without giving one person easy access today?

Inheritance and advanced recovery design may require professional legal and security advice in your location.

### Receiving bitcoin

Receiving is usually safer than sending because you do not authorize funds to leave. It still requires verification.

For an on-chain receipt:

1. Open the intended Bitcoin wallet.
2. Select **Receive**.
3. Confirm that the wallet is showing an on-chain Bitcoin address, not another network or Lightning.
4. Copy the address or display its QR code.
5. Share only the address or payment request. Do not share keys or a seed phrase.
6. If the sender copies the address, ask them to compare it with the address on your own trusted screen.
7. Confirm the expected amount and whether the sender will subtract any fee.
8. After broadcast, check the wallet for the pending transaction.
9. Wait for a suitable number of confirmations based on the amount and risk.

A wallet may generate a new address after each receipt. Old addresses usually remain valid, but new addresses improve privacy by making it harder for an observer to connect payments.

Seeing a transaction in a message or screenshot is not proof of payment. Verify it in your wallet or through a trusted node or block explorer. For meaningful amounts, wait until the transaction is confirmed in a block.

For a classroom demonstration, use test funds or a very small amount. You should be able to identify the receive screen, amount, method, and status even if no real payment is made.

### Sending bitcoin

Sending requires care because a valid Bitcoin transaction is usually irreversible.

Before an on-chain send:

1. Confirm the recipient and purpose through a trusted communication channel.
2. Ask for a fresh address from the recipient when possible.
3. Confirm that it is an on-chain Bitcoin address for the correct network.
4. Enter or scan the address and verify it after entry.
5. Enter the amount and confirm the unit: bitcoin, sats, or fiat equivalent.
6. Review the network fee and expected timing.
7. Read the final summary on the wallet or hardware-device screen.
8. If the process, address, or amount is unfamiliar, send a small test first.
9. Approve the transaction only after every field is correct.
10. Save the transaction ID if you may need to track or discuss the payment.

The **network fee** is paid to miners for including the transaction in a block. It is based mainly on transaction data and current demand for block space, not simply on the amount sent. A larger payment does not automatically require a larger fee.

Wallet fee estimates can be wrong or become outdated. A low fee may cause a transaction to wait. A high fee may waste funds. Some wallets support fee adjustment, but the methods vary and are not required knowledge for this Diploma.

Never send because someone creates urgency. Stop if the recipient changes the address unexpectedly, asks you to “verify” the payment with your seed phrase, or refuses to allow a small test.

**Spot question:** In a prepared wallet or simulation, what would you check before sending a transaction? How would you verify the address before approval?

### Verification, block explorers, and irreversible transactions

Bitcoin replaces some trust with the ability to verify.

A **transaction ID**, or **TXID**, is an identifier for an on-chain transaction. A **block explorer** is a tool that displays public Bitcoin data such as transaction status, fees, addresses, blocks, and confirmations.

To check a transaction with a block explorer:

1. Obtain the TXID from the sending wallet.
2. Open a trusted explorer that supports the correct Bitcoin network.
3. Search for the TXID.
4. Check the destination, amount, fee, and confirmation status.
5. Remember that an explorer is an interface. Independent verification through your own node provides stronger privacy and trust reduction.

A transaction with no confirmation has been broadcast but is not yet included in a block. Its status can still change. The first confirmation occurs when a miner includes it in a valid block. Each valid block added after that increases the work that would need to be redone to replace its history.

More confirmations increase settlement confidence. They do not make a bad address good or turn a scam into a safe payment. They only show that the transaction is deeper in the accepted history.

The suitable number of confirmations depends on risk. A small purchase may be treated differently from a large settlement. Your educator may explain local practice, but there is no universal number for every situation.

Block explorers create privacy considerations. Searching your own address or TXID can reveal to the explorer's operator that your device is interested in that transaction. Avoid publishing addresses or transaction details without a reason.

Bitcoin transactions are designed for final settlement. There is no central institution that can cancel a confirmed transaction because you made a mistake. A recipient can voluntarily return funds, but the network cannot force them to do so. Verification must happen before approval.

For a classroom activity, an educator may use a printed explorer screenshot or a historical transaction, such as the Pizza Day transaction, to show what public ledger data can and cannot prove. The activity should also ask what privacy information becomes visible.

### Safety checklist: seed phrases, address checks, scams, and starting small

Use this checklist before you depend on any Bitcoin tool.

**Protect secrets**

- Never share a seed phrase or private key.
- Keep seed backups offline and out of view.
- Do not store a seed phrase in photographs, cloud files, email, or messages.
- Do not enter recovery words after following a link or advertisement.
- Lock and update the device that holds a hot wallet.

**Verify destinations**

- Confirm whether the payment is on-chain or Lightning.
- Check the address or invoice on the trusted receiving screen.
- Compare after copy-and-paste; malware can replace clipboard addresses.
- Verify important addresses on a hardware-wallet display when available.
- Use a small test for an unfamiliar process.

**Recognize scam patterns**

- **Impersonation:** A scammer pretends to be support staff, an educator, a public figure, a friend, or a government official.
- **Urgency:** You are told to act now or lose access, miss a profit, pay a penalty, or face danger.
- **Guaranteed returns:** An offer promises easy, fixed, or risk-free profit.
- **Giveaway fraud:** You are asked to send bitcoin first to receive more later.
- **Recovery fraud:** Someone claims they can recover lost bitcoin if you provide words, keys, remote access, or an advance fee.
- **Fake application or update:** A copy of a real wallet steals credentials or creates attacker-controlled addresses.
- **Phishing:** A message or website copies a trusted brand to collect login details or seed words.
- **Romance or relationship manipulation:** A trusted-feeling contact slowly introduces an investment, emergency, or payment request.
- **Too-good-to-be-true offer:** The reward is unusually high while the explanation is vague or secret.

Any one of these patterns is a red flag for a potential scam. Pause, do not send money or share information, and verify independently before taking any action.

**Check software and services**

- Prefer well-established open-source wallet software that can be reviewed, while remembering that open source alone does not guarantee safety.
- Use the project's official website or verified application listing.
- Check the developer name, domain spelling, and release information.
- Be cautious with sponsored search results and direct-message links.
- Use strong, unique account passwords and two-factor authentication for custodial services.
- Understand the recovery process before depositing funds.

**Control the size of mistakes**

- Start with an amount you can afford to lose.
- Practice receiving before sending.
- Test backups before storing meaningful funds.
- Increase security and complexity slowly.
- Do not borrow money to experiment.

When uncertain, pause. A real opportunity will survive a careful check. A scam often depends on preventing one.

**Spot question:** What clues would help you spot a scam, and what is the safe response if you see one?

### Acquiring and exchanging bitcoin

## Pathway awareness, not financial advice

People obtain bitcoin in several ways. Each path creates different privacy, custody, price, fraud, legal, and operational risks. This information is for education only and is not financial, investment, legal, or tax advice.

**Earning:** A person may receive bitcoin for work, goods, or services. This can be direct and practical, but both parties must agree on the amount, timing, payment method, and local tax treatment.

**Peer-to-peer exchange:** Two people may exchange fiat money or goods for bitcoin directly, in person or through an online matching service. This can reduce dependence on a central exchange but creates counterparty, physical-safety, payment-reversal, and fraud risks.

**Centralized exchange or broker:** A company may sell bitcoin through an account. This can provide liquidity and a familiar payment path. It creates identity, custody, hacking, withdrawal, fee, banking, and jurisdiction risk.

**Bitcoin ATM or kiosk:** A machine may exchange cash or another payment for bitcoin. It can be convenient but may charge high fees, require identification, impose limits, or use an unfavorable rate.

**Community or merchant payment:** A person may receive bitcoin as a refund, gift, donation, or local payment. The parties must still verify the wallet, amount, and tax or accounting implications.

Before using any path, compare:

| Risk area | Questions to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Price | What exchange rate and spread are used? |
| Fees | What service, network, deposit, or withdrawal fees apply? |
| Custody | Who holds the bitcoin before and after the exchange? |
| Identity | What personal data is collected, stored, or shared? |
| Settlement | Can the fiat payment be reversed? When is the bitcoin final? |
| Withdrawal | Can bitcoin be sent to a wallet you control? Are there limits or delays? |
| Counterparty | How do you know the other party will perform? |
| Safety | Is an in-person meeting public and appropriate? Is support legitimate? |
| Law and tax | Is the path lawful where you live? What records are required? |

**Know your customer**, or **KYC**, describes identity checks a regulated service may require. KYC can support legal compliance and account recovery, but it creates a record connecting identity to financial activity. No-KYC paths may offer more privacy, but can involve greater counterparty risk, complexity, price differences, or legal uncertainty.

No path is automatically best in every location. Provider-specific instruction belongs in the local Custom block and must not be treated as a global recommendation.

### On-chain and Lightning payments

Bitcoin supports more than one payment path.

An **on-chain payment** is recorded directly in Bitcoin's blockchain. It uses block space, pays a miner fee, and gains confirmations. On-chain payments provide base-layer settlement and are useful when final settlement matters. During busy periods, fees or waiting times may increase.

The **Lightning Network** is a payment network built on top of Bitcoin. Participants use payment channels and route payments between one another without recording every individual payment as a separate on-chain transaction. Lightning is designed for fast, frequent, or small payments.

This is a layer distinction. Bitcoin's base layer prioritizes open verification and final settlement. Lightning uses Bitcoin as its foundation while making different trade-offs for speed and frequent payments. Faster layers do not replace the base rules; they depend on them.

| Feature | On-chain Bitcoin | Lightning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Payment request | Bitcoin address or on-chain payment request | Lightning invoice, offer, or supported Lightning address |
| Settlement path | Recorded in a block | Routed through payment channels; channels ultimately depend on Bitcoin |
| Typical use | Base settlement, larger or less frequent transfer | Fast, smaller, or frequent payment |
| Fee conditions | Depend on block-space demand and transaction data | Depend on routing, wallet, liquidity, and service design |
| Timing | Broadcast first, then confirmations | Usually completes or fails quickly |
| Main beginner risk | Wrong address, unsuitable fee, confirmation delay | Wrong payment method, wallet custody, liquidity or routing failure |

Lightning wallets can be custodial or self-custodial. A simple Lightning application may hold the user's funds. Another may let the user control keys but require more backup and channel management. Do not assume that “Lightning wallet” describes the custody model.

Before paying, confirm which network the recipient expects. An on-chain wallet cannot send directly to every Lightning invoice, and a Lightning wallet may not support every on-chain function. Some applications support both and may convert between them, with added fees or trust.

Detailed Lightning setup and live payment practice belong in the Custom block when local tools, connectivity, educator preparation, and learner safety allow.

Channel setup, routing operations, and liquidity management are advanced topics. A beginner needs to recognize that these factors can affect whether a Lightning payment succeeds and what it costs, but does not need to operate routing infrastructure in the Core Diploma.

### Review: what does self-custody responsibility mean?

Pause and consider what direct control would require from you: could you protect a recovery plan, distinguish safe receiving information from secrets, and verify a recipient, amount, network, and fee before sending? Self-custody gives you control of the keys, but it removes the provider who might reset access or reverse an error; custodial services make a different set of trust and risk trade-offs. The practical recap is simple: protect keys and backups, choose tools carefully, recognise scam signals, and keep mistakes small while learning.

#### Key takeaways

- A wallet manages the keys and information used to receive, check, and spend bitcoin; it does not store bitcoin inside a phone or device.
- Addresses and payment requests can be shared to receive bitcoin, but private keys and seed phrases must remain secret.
- A seed phrase is full recovery access for many wallets, so it must be recorded accurately, protected offline, and never entered into a website or shared with another person.
- Custodial services control the keys and may provide account recovery, while self-custody gives the user direct control and direct responsibility for recovery.
- Hot wallets are convenient for regular use; cold or hardware wallets can provide stronger key isolation but still depend on a safe backup.
- Before receiving or sending, check the correct network, address or invoice, amount, fee, and transaction status; a valid send is usually irreversible.
- Start with safe practice or small amounts, recognize scam signals, and pause when a request creates urgency, secrecy, or pressure.

### Lightning payments where locally appropriate

Lightning can make fast, small Bitcoin payments practical. It may be suitable for a merchant counter, repeated local payments, online services, or transfers where an on-chain fee would be too large in relation to the payment.

A local Lightning demonstration should identify:

- whether the wallet is custodial or self-custodial;
- how the wallet is funded;
- whether funding requires an on-chain transaction or a service;
- what backup or recovery protects the balance;
- what type of payment request is being used;
- what fees or limits apply;
- what happens when a payment fails;
- how the recipient confirms payment.

A **Lightning invoice** is a payment request created for a specific payment. It can include an amount, destination information, and an expiry time. Read the wallet's confirmation screen before paying.

A **Lightning address** is a human-readable identifier supported by some services. It may look like an email address, but it is a payment-routing convenience, not an email account. It depends on the provider or domain that supports it.

For a guided payment:

1. The receiver creates the correct request.
2. The sender scans or copies it.
3. The sender checks the recipient, amount, unit, and fee.
4. The sender approves only after the details are correct.
5. Both sides confirm the result in their own wallets.
6. The group explains the custody and failure risks.

Do not assume that a successful Lightning payment proves the wallet is safe for larger balances. Payment convenience and custody security are separate questions.

### Daily-life Bitcoin use

Bitcoin can function as money in several ways. Spending is only one of them.

**Earning:** A worker, creator, business, or community member receives bitcoin for value provided. Earning can avoid a separate purchase process, but the receiver must agree on pricing, volatility, records, and custody.

**Saving:** A person holds bitcoin for future use. Predictable supply may be attractive, but the fiat exchange value can fall sharply and key security becomes important.

**Receiving:** A person accepts a gift, donation, refund, remittance, or repayment. The receiver must provide the correct payment request and verify settlement.

**Sending:** A person transfers value to family, friends, a business, or an organization. They must compare payment path, speed, fee, privacy, and finality.

**Spending:** A person exchanges bitcoin for goods or services. They should understand the exchange rate, merchant policy, payment method, and local tax consequences.

**Pricing and accounting:** A person or business records value in bitcoin or sats. In many places, the fiat value at the time of payment is still needed for accounts or taxes.

**Donating:** A person supports an organization or individual directly. They should verify the recipient's payment request through a trusted source.

For any use, ask three questions:

1. What existing problem does Bitcoin address here?
2. Is Bitcoin better than the available alternative for this case?
3. What new responsibility or risk does Bitcoin create?

Avoid starting with the tool. Start with the need.

### Circular economies

A **Bitcoin circular economy** is a network in which people earn, spend, save, and exchange bitcoin within a community. Value circulates among workers, customers, merchants, producers, educators, and service providers instead of entering only through a one-time purchase and leaving through immediate conversion.

A circular economy can reduce dependence on distant financial services and create direct local relationships. It can also fail if there is little demand, poor education, unreliable tools, weak security, or pressure to use bitcoin without a real need.

Map a simple local cycle:

1. Who can earn bitcoin?
2. Where can they spend or use it?
3. Which merchants can pay a supplier or worker in bitcoin?
4. Which participants need to exchange to or from fiat?
5. Who provides education and technical support?
6. What happens when internet access fails?
7. What custody and price risks does each participant carry?

A healthy circular economy depends on voluntary participation. Merchants and workers should understand the payment, pricing, custody, accounting, and volatility risks. Adoption numbers alone do not show whether the network is useful.

The strongest local loop solves a real problem for more than one participant. It should not depend permanently on giveaways or on one organizer processing every payment.

### Merchant or community use cases

A merchant needs more than a receive button. The full payment process may include pricing, staff training, receipts, refunds, custody, conversion, accounting, taxes, device security, and support.

Before accepting Bitcoin, a merchant should decide:

- whether prices are displayed in local currency, sats, or both;
- whether payments use on-chain Bitcoin, Lightning, or both;
- whether the wallet is custodial or self-custodial;
- who can access the receiving device;
- how received funds are secured or moved;
- whether some funds are converted to local currency;
- how payment is confirmed at the point of sale;
- how refunds and mistakes are handled;
- what records local law requires;
- what staff do when the network or wallet fails.

Community use cases may include:

- paying local workers or creators;
- receiving donations transparently;
- transferring value between community groups;
- supporting a savings group with clearly defined custody;
- helping a remote worker receive international payment;
- connecting local producers and merchants;
- teaching safe wallet use;

Community custody requires special care. If one organizer holds funds for many people, that organizer becomes a custodian and a point of trust. Shared funds need transparent rules, records, access controls, and legal review where appropriate.

For each use case, define success in terms of the problem solved, not the number of transactions completed.

### Review: how can Bitcoin function as money in your community?

Complete this review individually, then compare your reasoning with your team or cohort. **Block 4 answer:** Bitcoin can function as money when people voluntarily use it to earn, save, receive, send, spend, price, or settle value. A useful local application begins with a real need, makes custody and costs clear, respects local law and safety, and gives learners enough knowledge to choose freely.

#### Key takeaways

- Bitcoin as money is local: the useful pathway depends on community needs, infrastructure, laws, costs, tools, language, and safety conditions.
- A Custom module should begin with a real problem or opportunity, then define the purpose, context, practice, safety limits, and evidence of learning.
- Local workshops should use a safe sequence: observe the process, practise with support, then explain or complete a defined task without exposing secrets.
- Before using a local tool or service, check its purpose, custody model, source, recovery process, privacy, costs, compatibility, support, and current local rules.
- On-chain and Lightning payments make different trade-offs in settlement, speed, fees, custody, and failure points; confirm which payment method a recipient expects.
- Local exchange pathways, KYC choices, merchant flows, and community use cases should be compared through their full costs, custody, privacy, safety, and legal trade-offs.
- A strong circular economy or community use case is voluntary, solves a real problem, protects participants, and does not make learners dependent on one service, organizer, or promise.

### A safety reminder

Remember these rules:

- A seed phrase or private key gives control of funds. Never share it.
- Verify the recipient, network, address or invoice, amount, and fee before sending.
- Bitcoin transactions are usually irreversible.
- Use a small test when the process is new.
- Do not act under pressure, fear, or promises of guaranteed profit.
- Verify wallet software and support contacts through independent sources.
- Understand who holds the keys.
- Test recovery safely before meaningful funds depend on it.
- Keep financial balances and security arrangements private.
- Laws, taxes, fees, and services change. Check current local information.

Education reduces risk but cannot remove it. If a situation is unclear, stop and learn more before acting.
