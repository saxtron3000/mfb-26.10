This document gathers all feedback, reflections, and comments about the previous version of *Bitcoin for Juniors*.

The goal is to support a refresh of the program launched in April 2026 by identifying useful modifications, errors, and opportunities to make the program better.

## Feedback

### Amantikir Teacher Report

- Play, humor, magic, juggling, music, and storytelling were repeatedly used to build trust and keep the classroom emotionally open. The report suggests these are not optional flourishes: they are functioning as classroom-management and engagement tools.
- The island-survival trade activity worked better when students had more room to be creative. The teacher expanded the object list from four to ten items and found that it encouraged participation and richer discussion.
- The trading lessons benefited from explicitly practicing polite trade language such as "Excuse me," "Please," and "Thank you." Communication norms should stay embedded in the activity rather than treated as a side note.
- A scarce "extra" card representing fire created a strong practical demonstration of supply and demand. Students already understood the concept better than expected, so the curriculum may be able to name and extend the idea sooner.
- Desk-based or grouped seating worked better than sitting in a circle for students who struggled with concentration. Clearly defined workspaces reduced distractions and improved classroom organization.
- Repetition across lessons was consistently valuable. The teacher repeatedly returned to prior concepts, and the report frames creative review as essential for students to internalize the material.
- The price lesson revealed a curriculum opportunity: price and value should probably be introduced together, or at least closer together, so students can contrast "what something costs" with "what something means to me." The teacher specifically suggested combining Lessons 1.3 and 1.4.
- Symbolic gifts such as the Brazil/Bitcoin keychains created excitement, rapport, and a concrete example of low-price/high-value. This could be deliberately tied into the price-versus-value lesson.
- The lessons on time, routine, work, and energy were skipped because the teacher judged them too theoretical and likely to reduce participation. This is a serious signal that those lessons may need a more concrete, playful, and activity-based redesign.
- The Three Little Pigs worked well as an anchor story for time preference. Visual aids, character cutouts, emotional narration, and familiar narrative structure made the abstract idea easier to grasp.
- Drawing high and low time preference examples gave the teacher useful evidence of understanding and created brief one-on-one assessment moments with each student.
- Active creation worked better than passive observation. The report explicitly notes that playful activities where students participate directly produce better interaction and learning outcomes.
- Playful homework could extend learning between classes. The teacher suggested take-home coloring or similar assignments connected to the concepts.
- Parent-involved homework could introduce the concepts to adults and help families understand what students are learning.
- Gamification is worth exploring: healthy competition, scores, rewards, and recognition could make progress visible and motivate students.
- In the money/market activity, giving each student only two cards felt limiting. The teacher suggested at least three cards per student.
- Marketplace logistics may work better with an even number of groups, allowing groups to trade in pairs simultaneously.
- For younger students estimating prices, educators should not immediately reject unrealistic answers. Let students place their estimate first, then guide them through playful discussion toward a more realistic price.

## Reflections

- The strongest activities seem to translate abstract monetary concepts into embodied classroom experiences: trading objects, hunting for money, placing items on a price/value graph, acting through a story, or drawing a concept.
- The report implies that emotional connection is part of the pedagogy. The teacher repeatedly links trust, joy, gifts, performance, and humor to student readiness to learn.
- Some concepts may be too abstract in their current sequence or format. When the teacher skipped several lessons, it was not because the concepts were unimportant, but because the delivery seemed mismatched to the students' developmental stage and classroom energy.
- Assessment opportunities appeared naturally when students created artifacts, especially drawings and sticky-note placements. The revised curriculum could intentionally include more visible student outputs.

## Comments

- Group size and seating arrangement materially affected lesson quality. Smaller attendance and structured desks made lessons smoother.
- Students from disadvantaged backgrounds may respond strongly to small symbolic objects when the gift carries care, identity, or belonging. This should be handled thoughtfully and not reduced to generic prizes.
- The report is teacher-centered and reflective, not a controlled comparison. Treat its conclusions as useful field feedback, especially where the same pattern appears across both groups.

## Task Seeds For `03_tasks.md`

These are source-grounded task ideas extracted from the review notes. The authoritative task status belongs in `03_tasks.md`.

- Review Lessons 1.3 and 1.4 and decide whether price and value should be combined or more tightly sequenced.
- Redesign the time, routine, work, and energy lessons to be more concrete, visual, and participatory before re-testing them.
- Add facilitator guidance on seating: use desks or defined group workspaces for classes with attention or classroom-management challenges.
- Add facilitator notes encouraging creative repetition of previous concepts at the start of each class.
- Preserve or expand story-based teaching for time preference, using visual props and student-created outputs.
- Add optional playful homework prompts, including a parent-involvement variant.
- Explore a lightweight gamification layer with progress recognition, while keeping competition healthy and cooperative.
- Update trading-game materials or instructions to recommend at least three cards per student when possible.
- Update marketplace setup guidance to prefer an even number of groups when group-to-group trading is used.
- Add educator guidance for price-estimation activities: validate the student's first attempt, then guide correction through discussion.
