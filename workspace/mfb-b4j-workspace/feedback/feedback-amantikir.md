### **Class 1 – Group 1 – 19/05/2026**

**(Ages 9 to 11\) (27 students, usually around 20 present)**

### **Lesson 1 – Introduction**

As this was the first lesson and the children’s first contact with us, my main objective was to create a sense of closeness and begin earning their trust. I believe that playing, smiling, and making wholesome jokes was a constructive way to achieve this.

Following the introductory lesson plan, I told the island survival story, which took about ten minutes. I invited them to imagine themselves as sailors, using vivid mental imagery and referencing beaches and places they likely knew so they could immerse themselves in and participate in the story.

After arriving on the island, I asked them to list ten objects they would need to survive for one week. Instead of limiting them to four objects as suggested in the curriculum, I felt that giving them more options would encourage greater creativity and expand the exercise. This worked very well.

We then discussed the qualities of each object, giving every student the opportunity to explain how it could be used on the island.

Next, the students paired up and I explained how trading would work. My two main objectives during this activity were:

1. **Kind communication:** What words should we use when speaking to someone we want to trade with? Together we identified expressions such as “Excuse me,” “Please,” and “Thank you.”  
2. **Recognising and communicating value:** How can I highlight the value of something I own in order to honestly persuade someone else to become interested in it? This also helped build confidence in what they have and who they are.

The students first traded in pairs and then participated in an open marketplace where everyone could trade with everyone else. Afterwards, I asked about the difficulties and successes they experienced to better understand how they approached the activity.

I also introduced one unique **EXTRA card** into the deck so they could experience the concept of supply and demand. This special card represented something extremely useful (fire), and everyone wanted to obtain it. Without explicitly using the terms “supply” and “demand,” I explained that when something is scarce and many people want it, it becomes more valuable.

To my surprise, the students already understood this concept and were able to explain exactly what supply and demand means.

I ended the lesson by performing some juggling to create a fun and memorable ending. I believe finishing each lesson with a playful activity helps strengthen student engagement.

**Concepts taught:**

* Exchange before money  
* Kind communication  
* Appreciating what we have and who we are  
* Supply and demand

### **Lesson 2 – 26/05/2026**

**Introduction to shells as one of the earliest forms of money**

We began by spending about ten minutes reviewing the concepts from the previous lesson.

Next, we recalled the six most important objects the students had selected for survival and wrote them on the board.

My assistant hid the “shells” (we actually used stones). I explained that throughout history people have used many different forms of money, including grains, fruit, and shells.

The class was divided into four groups of five students, and we played a treasure hunt where each team searched for the stones representing money.

One special stone (a crystal) was worth extra points.

The students thoroughly enjoyed searching for the stones, and the competition remained healthy and enthusiastic.

After counting each group’s stones, the activity changed. Instead of trading objects, the groups now bought and sold using their stones.

Afterwards, the students shared how much easier it was to buy something rather than rely on direct exchange.

We discussed the problem of the **double coincidence of wants**, explaining how money solves this challenge.

I also introduced three characteristics of good money:

1. Scalability  
2. Durability  
3. Usability (acceptability)

The students unanimously agreed that buying was easier than bartering.

**Concepts taught:**

* Teamwork  
* Kind and effective communication  
* Double coincidence of wants  
* Characteristics of good money

### **Lesson 3 – Group 1 – 02/06**

We began by reviewing concepts from previous lessons. I believe continuous repetition is extremely important for reinforcing students’ understanding of earlier material.

We initially worked in a circle, but I believe these students, who struggle with concentration, benefit more from remaining seated at their desks. This provides more structure and reduces distractions.

The lesson focused on **Price**.

We asked the question:

**What is price?**

The students answered: *The amount of money something costs.*

We brought pre-cut magazine pictures to save class time.

The students loved choosing pictures and placing them on a price scale ranging from “more expensive” to “cheaper.”

Most of them were already able to distinguish expensive items from inexpensive ones.

The activity was enjoyable.

From an educational perspective, however, I believe this lesson should introduce the concept of **Value** alongside **Price**, helping students immediately distinguish between the two concepts.

For this reason, I think Lessons 1.3 and 1.4 could potentially be combined into a single lesson.

We also surprised the students by giving each of them a small 3D-printed keychain featuring the Brazilian flag and the Bitcoin “B.”

For children from disadvantaged backgrounds, receiving a symbolic gift demonstrates that something relatively inexpensive can still hold tremendous value because of what it represents.

Since the World Cup had just begun, everyone was excited about Brazil, while the Bitcoin logo quietly continued planting the “orange seed.”

### **Lesson 4 – Group 1 – 09/06**

This time the students remained seated at their desks throughout the lesson, which significantly improved both focus and classroom organisation.

Only twelve students attended, and the smaller class size also contributed to a smoother lesson.

We began by reviewing the concept of **Price**, playing with both the word and its meaning.

We also reinforced the concept of supply and demand.

I see tremendous value in continually revisiting previous concepts because repetition, when presented creatively, seems essential for helping students truly internalise their learning.

The main topic of this lesson was **Value**:

“The importance something has to each individual.”

Using practical examples such as bicycles, video games, and a football one student had received as a gift, we discussed the things they personally value.

Each student then wrote two important items from their bedroom on sticky notes.

On the board, we created a graph with:

* a vertical axis representing **Value**  
* a horizontal axis representing **Price**

Students placed their sticky notes where they believed each item belonged.

An interesting observation was that many students considered their pillow to be extremely valuable despite its relatively low price. This clearly demonstrated that things we value are not necessarily expensive.

I concluded the lesson with a magic trick to interact with the students, which they thoroughly enjoyed.

**Lesson 5 \- Group 1  16/06/2026**

(I decided to skip the lessons covering time, routine, work, and energy; I realized the content would be too theoretical, which tends to dampen the children's participation and engagement in the activity, potentially making the class boring.) I then decided to move on to a very important topic that, coincidentally, was next in the curriculum:  
Time Preference. I prepared the lesson by printing out the story of the Three Little Pigs (I thought the parallel to the suggested theme was excellent), but I added more emotion to the storytelling. I also cut out the figures and characters from the story (Mama Pig, the three little pigs, the Big Bad Wolf, and the three built houses).  
I repeated the lesson format, revisiting whether they truly understood the difference between price and value. I began the class by telling the story in a playful way—using different voices for each character—and as the story unfolded, I placed images on the board to provide a visual element. They love learning with visuals.

We discussed the concept of low versus high time preference, and I also gave real-life examples at the end—like when we receive a bit of money for Christmas: do we rush out to spend it immediately, or do we consider something more important to do with it and save some?

Time preference is a topic I will cover again in the next class.

**Lesson 6 Group 1-  23/06/2026**

We returned to the story of the Three Little Pigs to revisit the concepts of high and low time preference. I decided to ask them to draw. When I asked if they liked drawing, almost everyone shouted, "YEEES\!" (Playful activities that get them involved—rather than just watching—yield better results in terms of interaction and learning outcomes.)

Note: I had been wondering if the knowledge I was sharing with them was having even the slightest impact, or if I was simply there to entertain them.

I asked them to divide their paper in two and draw an example of high time preference on one side and low time preference on the other; they could include themselves, other people, or animals in the drawings—whatever they wished. As they brought their drawings to me, I was able to have a one-on-one moment with each child; I realized just how important this interaction is for them and for understanding their learning progress. To my great surprise, they all clearly understood that a lower time preference enables us to achieve more solid results in the future; I felt a deep sense of relief and joy knowing that this knowledge is truly reaching these children's hearts and minds. To wrap up the lesson, I performed a cup magic trick to keep the atmosphere fun and joyful.

Observation : Another interesting thing that happened was that I asked the students to take the drawings they made in class home to color and bring back. I realized it would be highly beneficial to create "homework" assignments—playful, fun activities that keep them connected to their learning throughout the week. I also had the idea that some of these assignments could involve parents, introducing the concepts we are working on to the adults; this could spark their interest in personal development and give them insight into what their children are learning.

Observation : Another idea is: how can we gamify the experience? Turning learning into a form of "healthy competition" featuring scores, rewards, and recognition of their progress?

### **Lesson 2 – 02/06**

We reviewed the key concepts from the previous lesson:

* Exchange came before money.  
* The word **barter**.  
* Polite communication (“Excuse me,” “Thank you,” and “Please”).  
* The difficulty of trading when two people want different things (double coincidence of wants).  
* Supply and demand.

We revisited the cards and selected the six most useful objects for island survival.

The students were divided into three groups of six.

Each student received two cards, although I believe giving at least three cards would improve the activity.

We hid stones for the treasure hunt and included one special stone worth five reais.

After counting the stones, the groups participated in a marketplace where they bought and sold with one another.

In future lessons, having an even number of groups would allow simultaneous trading between pairs of groups.

The main question afterwards was:

“Was it easier or harder to use money instead of bartering?”

Most students answered that using money was easier, helping them understand the problem that money was created to solve.

We also gave them World Cup keychains, which brought great excitement and strengthened both our relationship with the students and their anticipation for future lessons.

### **Lesson 3 – 09/06**

We reviewed the concepts from the previous lesson, particularly supply and demand, which the students already understand quite well.

We introduced the concept of **Price** as:

“The amount of money something costs.”

The class was divided into three groups, and each group received two pre-cut magazine pictures.

Their task was to estimate how much each object cost.

Because these students are younger (8 to 10 years old), they generally have a less developed understanding of prices than the older group. Nevertheless, they completed the activity enthusiastically and enjoyed placing their pictures on the board according to their estimated prices.

One important observation for educators is not to immediately reject a child’s initial estimate. Instead, allow them to confidently place the object where they believe it belongs. Afterwards, through playful discussion, gently guide them toward a more realistic estimate if necessary.

As in the previous group, I finished the lesson with a magic trick to build rapport and strengthen our relationship with the students.
