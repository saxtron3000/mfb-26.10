# Bitcoin Diploma Design Brief

## Purpose

This document defines the design logic for the redesigned Bitcoin Diploma.

It answers:

- What principles should guide redesign decisions?
- What should remain fixed?
- What can adapt?
- What assumptions should be made explicit?
- What learner experience should the redesign create?
- What educator experience should the redesign support?
- What design risks need to be managed?
- What open design questions remain?

This document does not define project workstreams, milestones, implementation tasks, publishing deliverables, or operational completion criteria.

For workstreams and deliverables, see `03_scope.md`.

For learning outcomes and competencies, see `05_objectives.md`.

For assessment, see `13_assessment.md`.

For certification, see `15_certification.md`.

# 1. Design Aim

Design a shorter, clearer, more practical, more modular, and more teachable Bitcoin Diploma while preserving:

- educational seriousness;
- community-driven delivery;
- open-source accessibility;
- learner autonomy;
- educator empowerment;
- conceptual coherence;
- grassroots adaptability.

The redesign should improve the learning experience without replacing the identity, philosophy, or purpose of the Diploma.

Internal purpose research clarifies that the Diploma should remain a structured, foundational, globally accessible introduction to Bitcoin. It should use money, economics, monetary history, fiat-system critique, practical Bitcoin concepts, and sovereignty as connected material that helps learners reason independently about their financial future.

# 2. Working Design Direction

The current working design direction is:

- educator-facilitated delivery in person or online;
- approximately 8-hour minimum planning baseline, with additional time where objectives require it;
- working architecture of four blocks—Problem / Broken Money, Solution / Bitcoin, Action / Self-custody, and Custom / Bitcoin as Money—rather than fixed two-hour classes;
- single Diploma with Custom and Advanced pathways;
- Core / Custom / Advanced content separation;
- English-first drafting followed by explicit simplicity, tone, style, and translation-readiness review;
- practical learning increased substantially;
- practical skills structured using PPP: Presentation, Practice, Production;
- advanced technical content separated from the beginner pathway;
- Organizations material used only where it clarifies beginner Core concepts, educator misconception handling, Activity Bank scenarios, or local Custom practice;
- Educator Guide treated as a facilitation engine;
- Activity Bank treated as part of the learning ecosystem;
- assessment built from the learning objectives, using a theoretical component worth 50% (80% app-evaluated / 20% educator-evaluated) and a practical component worth 50% (80% direct educator observation / 20% app task follow-up);
- certification based on both theoretical evidence and directly observed practical competence;
- localization considered through adaptable examples, activities, and facilitation, not through changing core outcomes;
- low-tech, offline-capable print/PDF content treated as the first publication mode for in-person facilitation or online facilitation, with future branches to online PDF, online cohort, self-paced, web, an optional full-course app-delivery branch, and cross-modal continuation; the required assessment app is a current dependency rather than a future branch;
- print PDFs kept clean, with QR codes used sparingly for stable, high-value destinations;
- shared theory and Core outcomes standardized globally, while practice is localized and led by educators or local experts;
- an internal "racecar" standard: every component should earn its place by improving clarity, completion, practice, educator usability, or learner safety.

These are working design assumptions, not final curriculum specifications.

For implementation of these assumptions, see `03_scope.md`.

## 2.1 Clarified Design Notes

The following rough design notes are clarified into stable design rules:

| Rough note | Clarified design rule |
|---|---|
| Design for multi-lingual | Draft in English first, then edit the English source for simple language, consistent tone, clear style, and translation readiness before translation or publication. |
| Simple over technical | Prefer plain beginner explanations over technical detail unless the detail directly supports safety, reasoning, or later understanding. |
| Practical skills | Treat practical learning as guided skill orientation: demonstration, practice, production, reflection, and safe next steps. |
| Precise Learning Outcomes | Let measurable outcomes control content decisions. If an objective cannot be taught, practiced, and evidenced inside the beginner pathway, it should not be Core. |
| Include future ideas | Preserve future pathways in roadmaps, Advanced material, and publication planning without expanding the required beginner Diploma. |
| Forking off modes | Design from low-tech, offline-capable print/PDF teaching toward clear future instructional branches: online PDF, online cohort, self-paced, web, an optional full-course app-delivery branch, and eventual cross-modal continuation, while preserving the mandatory app assessment. |
| Don't put too many QR codes on PDF in print to start | Use QR codes sparingly in print. Prefer a few durable destinations over many fragile links that clutter pages or become stale. |
| Consensus based theory with localized, expert-led practice | Keep the Core theory layer and outcomes shared globally. Let local educators or experts select and adapt practice responsibly. |
| 4 units - total 8hrs? | Use four blocks, not old module labels, as the required structure. Plan from objectives and required evidence: four two-hour sessions are one useful format, not a limit. |
| We are building a racecar | Design for a lean, high-performance learning experience. Remove ornament, duplication, and content that slows completion without improving learning. |
| Problem / solution / self-custody / spending | Keep the official block sequence as Problem / Broken Money, Solution / Bitcoin, Action / Self-custody, and Custom / Bitcoin as Money. Spending is an important possible Block 4 practice, but Block 4 should not be narrowed globally to spending only. |

# 3. Design Tensions

The redesign responds to four current learner-experience tensions:

| Dimension | Current problem | Redesign response |
|---|---|---|
| Time | Time required is too high. | Shorten the pathway, clarify pacing, and reduce unnecessary content density. |
| Value | Perceived learner value is too low or too hard to reach. | Make value easier to experience through practical learning, clearer outcomes, and stronger local relevance. |
| Energy | The learning experience does not ask for enough active learner energy. | Increase discussion, practice, reflection, learner explanation, and production. |
| Autonomy | Unsupported beginner autonomy is too high. | Provide clearer sequencing, safer beginner boundaries, educator support, and guided pathways. |

The design goal is to reduce unnecessary burden while increasing meaningful participation and visible learner value.

# 4. Design Principles

## 4.1 Teach How To Think, Not What To Think

The Diploma should develop independent reasoning rather than passive agreement.

This principle is grounded in existing source material and internal stakeholder feedback. The Diploma should provide facts, trade-offs, and reasoning tools without requiring learners to adopt a predetermined conclusion.

Learners should be encouraged to:

- ask better questions;
- compare claims;
- reason from first principles;
- distinguish evidence from opinion;
- distinguish global curriculum content from local tool or service examples;
- continue learning independently.

## 4.2 Preserve Thematic Coherence

The redesigned Diploma should not become a collection of disconnected blocks.

Thematic consistency should be intentionally preserved across:

- money;
- economics;
- technology;
- Bitcoin;
- sovereignty;
- responsibility.

The learner should experience a coherent narrative rather than a disconnected sequence of lessons.

## 4.3 Preserve Conceptual Progression

The Diploma should continue moving from foundational concepts toward more advanced concepts.

Design decisions should avoid:

- introducing complexity too early;
- assuming hidden prerequisite knowledge;
- disrupting conceptual progression.

## 4.4 Design For Conversations

The Diploma should not simply transfer information.

It should intentionally create:

- conversations;
- reflection;
- dialogue;
- perspective comparison;
- critical thinking.

Learning experiences should generate structured discussion rather than merely deliver content.

## 4.5 Design For Independent Reasoning

The goal is not agreement.

The goal is independent thought.

Learners should leave with stronger reasoning capabilities rather than memorized conclusions.

## 4.6 Manage Cognitive Load

The redesign should reduce unnecessary cognitive load by:

- clarifying prerequisites;
- improving sequence;
- reducing duplication;
- separating beginner content from advanced content;
- clarifying technical depth;
- using clearer conceptual hierarchy.

The aim is not to oversimplify. The aim is improved learning efficiency.

## 4.7 Design For Safe Practical Orientation

The Diploma should help beginners identify safe next steps without overstating practical mastery.

Learners should encounter:

- wallet recovery concepts;
- seed phrase testing and protection;
- address verification;
- scam awareness;
- on-chain and Lightning distinctions;
- acquisition-pathway risks.

These topics should be framed as guided orientation, not as permission to manage high-stakes custody or make financial decisions independently.

## 4.7.1 Design For Valid Assessment

Assessment should match the learning objective and the kind of competence being claimed.

The app should deliver objective-aligned micro-learning, 80% of theoretical assessment, and the 20% practical-task follow-up, including knowledge checks, feedback, and progress tracking. Educators should gather the remaining 20% of theoretical evidence through approved objective-aligned interactions and evaluate 80% practical performance using common tasks and rubrics.

Blocks 1 and 2 should be knowledge-dominant, while Blocks 3 and 4 should be skills-dominant without excluding app evidence. The complete Diploma assessment allocates 50% to theory (80% app evaluation and 20% educator-evaluated interaction) and 50% to practice (80% direct observation by an educator and 20% app follow-up). Educator-led activities may be formative, but only approved objective-aligned interactions with criteria and records contribute to the theoretical score.

The app may record educator results and evaluate task-linked follow-up, but it cannot determine whether a learner can perform a practical skill. Certification requires both components and their required contributions.

## 4.8 Design For Multilingual Transfer

The Diploma should be drafted in English first and designed for later translation.

Translation affects tone, style, clarity, and meaning. Multilingual design therefore begins during English drafting and editing, not after the English version is finished.

The English source should use:

- simple, direct language;
- short sentences that usually carry one main idea;
- a clear, respectful, and consistent tone;
- active constructions where they improve clarity;
- consistent terminology;
- glossary alignment;
- defined technical terms;
- minimal idioms, slang, wordplay, and culturally narrow metaphors;
- separation between global concepts and local examples;
- language notes for difficult Bitcoin, money, custody, and safety terms.

Before translation or publication, the English source should pass a simplicity and translation-readiness review. The review should check:

- whether a beginner can understand the language without unnecessary technical knowledge;
- whether sentences can be shortened or made less ambiguous;
- whether the same concept uses the same term throughout;
- whether tone and emphasis can survive translation;
- whether idioms, jokes, metaphors, or cultural references need removal or explanation;
- whether examples should be translated directly, localized, or replaced;
- whether essential meaning depends on English-specific phrasing.

The editorial sequence should be:

1. Draft in English.
2. Edit for learning accuracy and beginner clarity.
3. Review English tone, style, simplicity, terminology, and translation readiness.
4. Approve the English source for translation.
5. Translate and localize.
6. Review the translated version for meaning, tone, simplicity, terminology, and local appropriateness.
7. Approve the relevant language version for publication.

Multilingual design does not mean all localized versions must be completed during the current redesign. It means the English source must be deliberately prepared for translation and must pass a simplicity review before publication.

## 4.9 Prefer Simple Over Technical

The beginner Diploma should privilege useful clarity over impressive detail.

Technical content belongs in Core only when it:

- unlocks beginner understanding;
- supports safer practice;
- prepares learners for a later concept;
- helps learners ask better questions.

Otherwise, technical depth should move to Custom, Advanced, educator background, or future pathways.

## 4.10 Design For Delivery-Mode Branching

The first content implementation should remain low-tech and offline-capable, with in-person and online facilitation supported. Certification still requires access to the assessment app.

The design should also make future branches possible:

- offline print or PDF;
- online PDF;
- online cohort;
- self-paced course;
- web version;
- required assessment-app integration;
- eventual cross-modal continuation across formats.

Each branch should preserve the same Core outcomes while adapting pacing, educator support, evidence of learning, accessibility, and technology assumptions.

## 4.11 Use Shared Theory And Local Expert Practice

The Diploma should distinguish between shared theory and local practice.

Shared theory includes:

- Core outcomes;
- beginner money, fiat, and Bitcoin explanations;
- safety principles;
- non-endorsement boundaries;
- assessment expectations.

Local expert practice includes:

- tool demonstrations;
- spending and merchant scenarios;
- remittance, nonprofit, donation, underbanked-access, and community payment scenarios when locally relevant;
- local payment rails;
- local on-ramp and off-ramp realities;
- legal, tax, privacy, and safety context;
- community-specific examples.

This distinction protects global coherence while allowing serious local usefulness.

## 4.12 Design For Print Restraint

Printed PDFs should be durable, clean, and usable offline.

QR codes should be used only for stable, high-value destinations such as:

- a source repository;
- a canonical update page;
- educator resources;
- donation or support pages;
- certificate verification, if available.

QR codes should not be scattered through the learner path as a substitute for essential printed content.

## 4.13 Maintain A Lean Performance Standard

"We are building a racecar" is an internal shorthand for design discipline.

Every component should improve at least one of the following:

- learner clarity;
- completion likelihood;
- practical competence;
- educator usability;
- safety;
- translation readiness;
- long-term maintainability.

If content does not improve one of these, it should be shortened, moved, or removed from the beginner pathway.

# 5. Non-Negotiables

The redesigned Diploma must:

- remain open source;
- remain community driven;
- preserve the identity of the Diploma;
- preserve conceptual and narrative coherence;
- preserve serious treatment of Bitcoin;
- support independent educators;
- support grassroots delivery;
- preserve accessibility;
- preserve learner autonomy;
- reduce unnecessary cognitive load;
- maintain learning quality while reducing duration;
- keep learning objectives standardized;
- keep Core theory and outcomes shared globally;
- draft source material in English first and prepare it deliberately for translation and localization;
- require an English-language simplicity, tone, style, and translation-readiness review before translation or publication;
- avoid unnecessary beginner technical overload;
- distinguish Core, Custom, and Advanced content;
- preserve the official four-block naming and avoid narrowing Block 4 globally to spending only;
- avoid global endorsement of named tools, services, wallets, exchanges, or local resources;
- fact-check or date current-data examples before publication;
- use QR codes sparingly in print PDFs;
- preserve low-tech, offline-capable content delivery for in-person teaching and online teaching while stating the mandatory app requirement for theory assessment and practical-task follow-up;
- support real-world implementation, not ideal conditions.

# 7. Technology Assumptions

The Diploma should be designed for varied technology contexts.

The first content-delivery mode should remain usable through in-person teaching or online teaching and offline-capable print or PDF materials. This does not create a device-free certification route: every learner must receive supported access to the required app theory assessment and practical-task follow-up.

Future delivery branches should be planned as adaptations of the same source, not as separate curricula with drifting outcomes.

Design questions:

- Can teaching and preparation occur offline while required app assessment access is scheduled separately?
- Can activities be delivered with minimal connectivity?
- Can learning occur with only mobile devices?
- What happens when technology fails?
- What assumptions are being made about internet access?
- Which content must remain printable?
- Which links are important enough for QR codes?
- Which future modes need different evidence of learning?

Technology should support learning. It should not become a hidden prerequisite.

Implementation and contingency planning belong in `03_scope.md`.

# 8. Adaptation Boundaries

The redesigned Diploma should distinguish between fixed, adaptable, and flexible-with-guardrails elements.

## Fixed

- Learning objectives
- Core outcomes
- Competency expectations
- Objective-to-assessment blueprint
- Two-part assessment requirement
- Minimum evidence and rubric criteria for certification
- Bitcoin-only educational focus
- Four-block beginner architecture
- Shared Core theory and safety boundaries

## Adaptable

- examples;
- case studies;
- activities;
- named tools and services;
- current local resources;
- classroom structures;
- timing adjustments;
- facilitation style;
- local references;
- language examples;
- spending and merchant scenarios;
- local practice modules.

## Flexible With Guardrails

- delivery pacing and session grouping;
- delivery mode adaptations;
- localization choices;
- legal, tax, privacy, and safety context;
- depth of Custom and Advanced material;
- QR code use;
- future online, web, app, or cross-modal publication branches.

Adaptation should increase local relevance without weakening learning standards.

2. Practice

# 14. Design Risks

## Fragmentation Risk

Modularization may improve flexibility while weakening narrative coherence.

## Over-Optimization Risk

Reducing duration may reduce reflection, discussion, or competence if handled poorly.

## Educator Friction Risk

If the redesign increases educator workload, adoption may decrease.

## Infrastructure Risk

Activities that depend on connectivity, devices, or software may fail in some contexts.

## Endorsement Risk

Named tools, services, wallets, exchanges, maps, or local resources may be mistaken for global recommendations if not framed carefully.

## Current-Data Risk

Statistics, fees, legal context, provider availability, and network conditions may change before publication or localization.

## Outcome Drift Risk

Adaptation and localization may gradually weaken learning outcomes.

## Assessment Validity And Educator Variation Risk

App evaluation may over-reward recall, while educator theory and practical judgments may vary across cohorts. The 50/50 theoretical-practical allocation, internal 80/20 splits, a complete objective-aligned app blueprint, approved educator theory interactions, direct practical observation, task-linked app follow-up, common rubrics, educator calibration, reassessment rules, and moderation are required to protect certificate consistency.

## Translation Risk

English-first phrasing, complex sentences, inconsistent tone, idioms, inconsistent terms, or culturally narrow examples may distort meaning or make multilingual publication harder than necessary. Treating translation as a final production step may preserve avoidable problems from the English source.

## Delivery-Mode Drift Risk

Online, self-paced, web, app, or cross-modal versions may drift away from the shared Core outcomes if delivery-mode branches are not governed by the same source of truth.

## Print Clutter Risk

Too many QR codes, links, or online dependencies may weaken the printed learner experience and create stale publication artifacts.

## Local Practice Risk

Locally useful practice may become unsafe or misleading if it is not led by educators or local experts who understand tools, risks, laws, privacy, and learner readiness.

## Narrow Spending Risk

Reducing Block 4 to "spending" may understate broader local Bitcoin-as-money possibilities such as earning, saving, merchant discovery, circular economies, remittances, resource mapping, and community problem-solving.

## Organizations Source Creep Risk

The retiring Bitcoin for Organizations pathway contains strong material for misconceptions, payments, public-ledger analysis, policy, treasury, risk management, nonprofits, technology, and sector impacts. Most of that material assumes advanced organizational reasoning. It should not pull the beginner Diploma toward business strategy, compliance, investment, legal, tax, treasury, or institutional custody instruction.

# 17. Public Design Message

The public design message should be simple:

The Bitcoin Diploma is becoming shorter, clearer, more practical, and more locally adaptable while preserving its open-source, community-led identity.

Public communication should emphasize:

- The mission is not changing.
- The learner pathway is becoming easier to complete.
- The beginner pathway is organized into four blocks.
- Educators receive better tools and clearer facilitation support.
- Communities gain local relevance through the Custom / Bitcoin as Money block.
- Spending can be included as local practice, but Block 4 is broader than spending.
- Advanced pathways remain available without overloading beginners.

The design story should avoid excessive internal terminology. Use concrete contrasts such as long to short, dense to clear, lecture-heavy to active, centralized design to educator choice, and one document to learning ecosystem.
