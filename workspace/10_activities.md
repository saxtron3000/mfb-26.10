# Bitcoin Diploma Activity Bank

## Purpose

This document defines the Activity Bank for the redesigned Bitcoin Diploma.

Activities are removed from the main student-facing text so educators can choose, adapt, and facilitate them more easily.

The Activity Bank is designed for two uses. Activities can complement the Bitcoin Diploma by giving educators modular exercises connected to the diploma blocks, objectives, and competencies. They can also stand alone as short learning experiences for social gatherings, community events, conferences, meetups, workshops, or other public learning spaces.

A standalone activity should provide enough context, instructions, debrief questions, and safety notes for participants to learn a clear idea even if they have not completed the full diploma.

The Activity Bank contains educator-led formative activities, approved educator-evaluated theory interactions, and educator-observed practical competency tasks. Explanations, comparisons, discussions, games, scenarios, and role-plays support learning and formative feedback unless approved for the 20% theoretical evidence contribution. Formal practical certification tasks supply the 80% directly observed practical contribution and must link to the app's 20% task follow-up; they must use the approved objective, instructions, observable criteria, rubric, and safety conditions.

| 8.1 | Generate a SHA-256 Hash | Web experiment | Learners enter words, sentences, or other data into a SHA-256 generator and compare outputs to observe fixed length, one-way behavior, and the effect of small input changes. |

| 10.2 | Do You Have the Right to Control Your Own Money? | Guided reflection | Learners answer review questions connecting monetary rights, barter, monetary history, fixed supply, Bitcoin's creation, custody, Lightning, nodes, empowerment, and community contribution. |

## Internal educator feedback: activity pilot priorities

The November 2024 teacher survey is directional internal evidence, not a representative activity evaluation. Eight educators responded, and the form forced some respondents to mark skipped activities even when they used all of them. See `02_research.md` for the evidence note and limitations.

Use it to prioritize activity development and pilots as follows:

- Pilot and refine safe, objective-aligned wallet setup or prepared-wallet exercises, money-property discussions, and beginner ledger-exploration activities; these received the strongest positive mentions.
- Re-test consensus, barter, video, hashing, Lightning relay, and transaction role-play activities before recommending them broadly. Check whether the problem is the activity itself, educator confidence, materials, timing, or learner context.
- Do not make video, an external website, or a named explorer a required dependency. Every such activity must provide an equivalent print, diagram, prepared screenshot, spoken, or facilitator-led version.
- Do not promote a legacy activity solely because it is familiar or frequently used. Keep, revise, or retire it based on objective alignment, learner evidence, safety, accessibility, time, and pilot feedback.

# Activity Bank Principles

Activities should:

- maximize in-class time;
- support active learning;
- align to objectives;
- identify stable objective IDs;
- be easy for educators to facilitate;
- support Presentation, Practice, Production where practical skills are involved;
- support claim / evidence / trade-off reasoning where learners compare Bitcoin claims or misconceptions;
- include low-tech alternatives;
- work from offline or low-connectivity assumptions when possible;
- be adaptable across communities;
- include language or translation notes where terminology may not transfer cleanly;
- identify when local expert guidance is needed;
- avoid unsafe financial or custody assumptions.

Each published Activity Bank item must also state its realistic facilitation time, whether it has been piloted, its online and offline suitability, any required materials or tool dependencies, and an equivalent low-tech fallback. Named websites, explorers, or trackers may be listed only as optional examples; they must not be a required dependency or a global recommendation.

Every activity must be identified as one or more of:

- learning and practice;
- formative assessment; or
- summative educator-evaluated theory interaction for certification; or
- summative practical assessment for certification.

Educators may adapt ordinary learning and formative activities within the published guardrails. A summative practical assessment task may be adapted only through an approved equivalent version that preserves the objective, evidence, difficulty, rubric, and safety standard.

Learners should receive supported practice before a task is used for summative practical assessment.

## Activity Title

### Block Alignment

Problem / Broken Money, Solution / Bitcoin, Action / Self-custody, or Custom / Bitcoin as Money.

### Objective Alignment

List the stable objective ID or IDs and the exact measurable objective.

### Competency Alignment

List the competency or competencies the activity develops or assesses.

### Assessment Use

Learning and practice, formative assessment, summative educator-evaluated theory interaction, summative practical assessment, or a permitted combination.

### Assessment Component

Formative theoretical support, summative educator-evaluated theory interaction, summative practical competency assessment, or formative preparation plus approved summative criteria.

### Educator

For a formative item, identify the educator or facilitator. For a summative educator-theory item, identify the educator and its approved criteria/record. For a summative practical item, identify the educator. The app supplies the complementary 80% theoretical evidence and practical-task follow-up under `14_microlearning.md`.

### Time

Estimated minutes.

### Delivery Mode

In-person, offline print/PDF, online cohort, self-paced, web-supported, or adaptable.

### Materials

Required materials and low-tech alternative.

### Educator Setup

Preparation steps.

### Learner Task

What learners do.

### Observable Criteria

What the learner must say, decide, illustrate, or demonstrate. For a practical task, list the required sequence, verification behaviors, explanations, safety behaviors, and stop conditions.

### Debrief

Questions that connect the activity back to the block objective.

### Evidence Of Learning

For formative use, state what the educator can notice and use for feedback. For summative educator-theory use, state the individual response, criteria, and concise record. For summative practical use, state what the educator must observe and the linked app follow-up.

### Rubric And Passing Standard

Define **Meets standard** and **Not yet**, including any safety-critical error that prevents a pass.

### Feedback And Reassessment

For formative theory, state how the educator gives feedback and directs further learning. For summative educator-theory work, state how the educator gives feedback and reassesses the approved interaction. For summative practical work, state how the educator gives feedback, what practice follows a **Not yet** result, how the learner can be reassessed against the same rubric, and how the linked app follow-up is retried.

### Evidence Record

For a summative practical task, state what result, evidence reference, or educator attestation must be recorded and where. Formative notes are not certification gates.

### Adaptation Notes

Local examples, language notes, device assumptions, and accessibility considerations.

For a summative task, explain which adaptations preserve equivalent evidence and which changes require approval.

### Local Expert Notes

Local legal, tax, privacy, safety, tool, or community knowledge required before facilitation.

# Potential Discussion: Competency Questions Across The Diploma

### Block Alignment

Problem / Broken Money, Solution / Bitcoin, and Action / Self-custody.

### Objective Alignment

`B1.1`, `B2.2`, `B2.3`, `B3.4`, `B3.6`, `B3.7`, and `ALL.1`.

### Competency Alignment

Monetary foundations, Bitcoin fundamentals, practical Bitcoin capability, guided critical discussion, and social learning.

### Assessment Use

Learning and practice or formative assessment. These prompts may inform a formal assessment task, but they are not a summative task by themselves.

### Assessment Component

Theoretical learning and formative checking, with practical prompts used to prepare for educator-observed practical competency assessment.

### Educator

Educator.

### Time

10–20 minutes, or one question as a short transition or review.

### Delivery Mode

In-person, online cohort, offline print/PDF, or adaptable.

### Materials

The relevant Diploma section. For practical prompts, use a prepared wallet, printed wallet screens, or a simulation; never use a learner's real recovery information.

### Educator Setup

Select questions that match material learners have already studied and practised. Decide whether learners will answer individually, in pairs, or as a group. For a practical question, prepare a safe simulation and the observable criteria before beginning.

### Learner Task

Invite learners to answer a question in their own words, give a reason or example, and respond respectfully to a different explanation. Use these potential questions:

| Feedback level | Potential discussion question |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Why did money come into existence? |
| 2 | Why does Bitcoin have a limited supply? |
| 3 | How would you send a transaction? |
| 3 | How would you recover a wallet? |
| 4 | What would help you spot a scam? |
| 4 | How would you verify an address before sending? |
| 5 | Why does a node verify transactions? |

### Observable Criteria

Look for an explanation or action that matches the relevant objective. For example, a learner should connect money to exchange and coordination; connect Bitcoin's supply limit to public rules and node verification; identify relevant safety checks before sending; and explain that nodes reject transactions or blocks that break Bitcoin's rules. A learner responding to a recovery prompt must protect recovery information throughout.

### Debrief

Ask: What evidence or check supports your answer? What could go wrong? What would make you stop or seek help?

### Evidence Of Learning

The educator may record concise notes on the learner's explanation, safety judgment, or simulated action for formative feedback. For certification, an educator may collect the approved 20% theoretical evidence through an objective-aligned interaction with criteria and a concise record; an educator collects the directly observed practical evidence through an approved practical task and rubric.

### Adaptation Notes

Translate the questions for plain local language without changing the expected concept or action. Do not substitute date-recall questions such as “In what year did this happen?” for explanation, reasoning, or performance evidence unless a date is needed to explain the concept's context.

### Safety Notes

Do not ask learners to reveal a seed phrase, private key, login credential, or personal transaction history. A spoken answer to a send or recovery question does not replace direct observation by an educator when the objective requires demonstration.

# Assessment Role By Block

## Blocks 1 And 2: Knowledge-Dominant

The mandatory app supplies 80% of theoretical evidence through objective-aligned microlearning and evaluation. Approved Activity Bank explanations, comparisons, concept illustrations, games, discussions, simulations, scenarios, and role-plays may supply the remaining 20% when they have an objective, individual evidence, criteria, and record; otherwise they are formative.

Selected educator-led theoretical activities should be short and objective-aligned; the blueprint must distinguish the approved summative 20% from formative touchpoints. The Activity Bank owns approved tasks for 80% of the practical 50%, which an educator evaluates through direct observation, and links them to the app's 20% practical follow-up.

## Blocks 3 And 4: Skills-Dominant

Activity Bank assessment tasks provide the direct educator-observed 80% of evidence required for practical competence and identify the linked 20% app follow-up.

Formal tasks should include approved variations of:

- wallet navigation;
- receive-information location and verification;
- simulated or appropriately low-stakes send or receive flows;
- transaction-status or confirmation checks;
- recovery-readiness and secret-protection scenarios;
- scam, address-error, or irreversible-transaction responses; and
- selected local Block 4 payment, receiving, remittance, merchant, donation, resource, or community-use performance.

An app checklist or follow-up may prepare a learner, capture task understanding, or support an educator's procedure. It is not proof of practical competence without the required direct observation.

Every Block 4 certification assessment task must map to shared objectives `B4.1` and `B4.2`, even when the local tool or scenario differs.

# Organizations-Derived Activity Seeds

These seeds come from `advanced/mergefromorganizations.md`. They should be developed as Activity Bank items only if they remain beginner-safe and locally adaptable.

## Claim, Evidence, Trade-Off

Learners choose a common claim, such as "Bitcoin is too slow," "CBDCs will replace Bitcoin," "Bitcoin is not decentralized," or "there are thousands of other coins." They identify what evidence would be needed, name one real trade-off, and explain what remains uncertain.

Keep the activity focused on reasoning. Do not turn it into a debate about price, politics, or investment advice.

## Explore The Ledger

Learners inspect a transaction using a live block explorer, a local node interface, or a printed screenshot. They identify amount, fee, confirmation status, and visible public information, then name one privacy risk created by searching or sharing transaction data.

Pizza Day may be used as a historical example if the educator verifies the source and avoids turning the activity into a price speculation exercise.

## Address History And Reuse Lookup

Using a deliberately public, educator-prepared address, transaction, local-node view, or printed screenshot, learners identify visible address activity and decide whether the address appears to have been reused. They discuss how repeated use can make payments easier to link and why a wallet may provide a fresh receiving address. The educator must not ask learners to search, display, or share their own address or anyone else's address. Use a printed or projected example when connectivity is unavailable.

## Local Payment Path Comparison

Learners compare an existing local payment method with an on-chain Bitcoin path and, where appropriate, a Lightning path.

Comparison fields:

- total amount received;
- time;
- fees and spreads;
- custody;
- privacy;
- reversibility;
- failure points;
- local law or support needs.

## Community Donation Scenario

Learners design a safe donation flow for a local nonprofit, classroom project, community group, or simulated organization.

The task should identify:

- how donors verify the payment request;
- who controls the wallet;
- what transparency is useful;
- what donor information should remain private;
- what records may be required locally;
- what failure or scam risks must be controlled.
